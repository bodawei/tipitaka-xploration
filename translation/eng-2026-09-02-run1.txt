Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, verses 104-123

[104] A "field" is where grain crops or later crops grow. "Goods situated in a field" means goods deposited in a field in one of four positions: resting on the ground, resting on a surface, suspended in the air, resting aloft. If, with intent to steal, thinking "I will take away goods situated in a field," he looks for an accomplice or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches it: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it shift: a grave offence. He moves it from its place: an offence entailing defeat. If, with intent to steal, he touches grain crops or later crops grown there worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes them shift: a grave offence. He moves them from their place: an offence entailing defeat. He lays claim to the field: an offence of wrong-doing. He causes doubt in the owner: a grave offence. When the owner gives up his claim, thinking "It will not be mine": an offence entailing defeat. Going to law, he defeats the owner: an offence entailing defeat. Going to law, he is defeated: a grave offence. He shifts a stake or a cord or a fence or a boundary marker: an offence of wrong-doing. When one act still remains to be done: a grave offence. When that act has been done: an offence entailing defeat.

[105] "Site" means the site of a monastery park, the site of a dwelling. "Goods situated on a site" means goods deposited on a site in one of four positions: resting on the ground, resting on a surface, suspended in the air, resting aloft. If, with intent to steal, thinking "I will take away goods situated on a site," he looks for an accomplice or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches them: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes them shift: a grave offence. He moves them from their place: an offence entailing defeat. He lays claim to the site: an offence of wrong-doing. He causes doubt in the owner: a grave offence. When the owner gives up his claim, thinking "It will not be mine": an offence entailing defeat. Going to law, he defeats the owner: an offence entailing defeat. Going to law, he is defeated: a grave offence. He shifts a stake or a cord or a fence or a wall: an offence of wrong-doing. When one act still remains to be done: a grave offence. When that act has been done: an offence entailing defeat.

[106] "Goods situated in a village" means goods deposited in a village in one of four positions: resting on the ground, resting on a surface, suspended in the air, resting aloft. If, with intent to steal, thinking "I will take away goods situated in a village," he looks for an accomplice or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches them: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes them shift: a grave offence. He moves them from their place: an offence entailing defeat.

[107] "Wilderness" means that which is owned by people — that is wilderness. "Goods situated in the wilderness" means goods deposited in the wilderness in one of four positions: resting on the ground, resting on a surface, suspended in the air, resting aloft. If, with intent to steal, thinking "I will take away goods situated in the wilderness," he looks for an accomplice or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches them: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes them shift: a grave offence. He moves them from their place: an offence entailing defeat. If, with intent to steal, he touches wood or creepers or grass growing there worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it shift: a grave offence. He moves it from its place: an offence entailing defeat.

[108] "Water" means water in a vessel, or in a lotus pond, or in a reservoir. If with intent to steal he touches it: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He moves it from its place: an offence entailing defeat. Having put in his own vessel, if with intent to steal he touches water worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He gets it into his own vessel: an offence entailing defeat. He breaches an embankment: an offence of wrong-doing. Having breached the embankment, he lets out water worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence entailing defeat. He lets out water worth more than one māsaka but less than five māsakas: a grave offence. He lets out water worth one māsaka or less than one māsaka: an offence of wrong-doing.

[109] A "tooth-cleaning stick" is one that is cut or not cut. If with intent to steal he touches one worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it shift: a grave offence. He moves it from its place: an offence entailing defeat.

[110] A "forest tree" means a tree owned and used by people. If with intent to steal he cuts it, then for each stroke there is an offence of wrong-doing. When one stroke still remains: a grave offence. When that stroke has been given: an offence entailing defeat.

[111] "Portable goods" means goods carried by another. If with intent to steal he touches them: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes them shift: a grave offence. He moves them from their place: an offence entailing defeat. Thinking "I will lead him away on foot together with the goods he is carrying," he makes the first step: a grave offence. He makes the second step: an offence entailing defeat. Thinking "I will take the goods when they fall," he causes them to fall: an offence of wrong-doing. If with intent to steal he touches the fallen goods worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes them shift: a grave offence. He moves them from their place: an offence entailing defeat.

[112] A "deposit" means goods placed in trust. When told "Give me my goods," he says "I did not take them": an offence of wrong-doing. He causes doubt in the owner: a grave offence. When the owner gives up his claim, thinking "He will not give them to me": an offence entailing defeat. Going to law, he defeats the owner: an offence entailing defeat. Going to law, he is defeated: a grave offence.

[113] A "customs post" is one established by the king at a mountain pass, or a river ford, or a village gate, with the words: "Let them take a toll from whoever enters here." If, having entered there, with intent to steal he touches goods liable to the king's toll worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes them shift: a grave offence. He takes the first step past the customs post: a grave offence. He takes the second step past: an offence entailing defeat. Standing inside the customs area, he throws the goods outside the customs area: an offence entailing defeat. He evades the toll: an offence of wrong-doing.

[114] "A living being" here means a human being. If with intent to steal he touches such a one: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes him move: a grave offence. He moves him from his place: an offence entailing defeat. Thinking "I will lead him away on foot," he makes the first step: a grave offence. He makes the second step: an offence entailing defeat. "Footless creatures" means snakes and fish. If with intent to steal he touches one worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He moves it from its place: an offence entailing defeat.

[115] "Two-footed creatures" means human beings and birds. If with intent to steal he touches one: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He moves it from its place: an offence entailing defeat. Thinking "I will lead it away on foot," he makes the first step: a grave offence. He makes the second step: an offence entailing defeat.

[116] "Four-footed creatures" means elephants, horses, camels, oxen, donkeys, and cattle. If with intent to steal he touches one: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He moves it from its place: an offence entailing defeat. Thinking "I will lead it away on foot," he makes it move the first foot: a grave offence. He makes it move the second foot: a grave offence. He makes it move the third foot: a grave offence. He makes it move the fourth foot: an offence entailing defeat.

[117] "Many-footed creatures" means scorpions, centipedes, and caterpillars. If with intent to steal he touches one worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He moves it from its place: an offence entailing defeat. Thinking "I will lead it away on foot," he makes it move: for each step a grave offence. He makes it move the last foot: an offence entailing defeat.

[118] A "spy" is one who, having spied out goods, reports: "Take away such-and-such goods" — an offence of wrong-doing. The other takes away those goods: an offence entailing defeat for both. A "keeper of stolen goods" is one who guards goods that have been brought in: if with intent to steal he touches goods worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes them shift: a grave offence. He moves them from their place: an offence entailing defeat. "Theft by arrangement" means that several have made an arrangement and one of them takes away the goods: an offence entailing defeat for all of them.

[119] "Making an appointment" means that he makes an appointment saying: "Take away those goods before the meal, or after the meal, or at night, or by day, at that appointed time" — an offence of wrong-doing. The other takes away those goods at that appointed time: an offence entailing defeat for both. If he takes away those goods before or after that appointed time, there is no offence for the instigator; for the one who takes them away there is an offence entailing defeat.

[120] "Making a sign" means that he makes a sign, saying: "I shall wink my eye, or raise my eyebrow, or lift my head — at that sign take away those goods" — an offence of wrong-doing. The other takes away those goods at that sign: an offence entailing defeat for both. If he takes away those goods before or after that sign, there is no offence for the instigator; for the one who takes them away there is an offence entailing defeat.
