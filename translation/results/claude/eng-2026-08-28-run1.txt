Pārājikapāḷi — Verañjakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, verses 1-20

[1] At that time the Buddha, the Blessed One, was staying at Verañjā at the foot of Naḷeru's nimb tree, together with a great community of monks, some five hundred monks. Now the brahmin of Verañjā heard: "Indeed, sirs, the ascetic Gotama, the son of the Sakyans, who went forth from the Sakyan clan, is staying at Verañjā at the foot of Naḷeru's nimb tree together with a great community of monks, some five hundred monks. And of that Master Gotama this fine report has spread abroad: 'He is indeed the Blessed One, an arahant, perfectly enlightened, endowed with knowledge and conduct, well-gone, knower of the worlds, unsurpassed trainer of persons to be tamed, teacher of gods and humans, enlightened, blessed. Having realized by his own direct knowledge this world with its gods, its Māras and its Brahmās, this generation with its ascetics and brahmins, its rulers and people, he makes it known. He teaches the Dhamma that is good in the beginning, good in the middle, good in the end, with its meaning and its phrasing; he reveals the holy life that is utterly complete and pure. Good indeed it is to see such arahants.'"

[2] Then the brahmin of Verañjā approached the Blessed One; having approached, he exchanged greetings with the Blessed One. Having exchanged courteous and cordial talk, he sat down to one side. Seated to one side, the brahmin of Verañjā said this to the Blessed One: "I have heard this, Master Gotama: 'The ascetic Gotama does not pay homage to brahmins who are old, aged, elderly, advanced in years, come to the last stage of life, nor does he rise up for them or invite them to a seat.' Is this so, Master Gotama? For indeed Master Gotama does not pay homage to brahmins who are old, aged, elderly, advanced in years, come to the last stage of life, nor rise up for them, nor invite them to a seat. This, Master Gotama, is simply not proper." "Brahmin, I do not see anyone in this world with its gods, its Māras and its Brahmās, in this generation with its ascetics and brahmins, its rulers and people, to whom I should pay homage, or for whom I should rise up, or whom I should invite to a seat. For, brahmin, if the Tathāgata were to pay homage to anyone, or rise up for him, or invite him to a seat, that man's head would split apart."

[3] "Master Gotama is one who lacks taste." "There is, brahmin, a way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama lacks taste.' For, brahmin, those tastes of forms, tastes of sounds, tastes of odours, tastes of flavours, tastes of tangibles—these have been abandoned by the Tathāgata, cut off at the root, made like a palm stump, done away with, so that they are not subject to future arising. This, brahmin, is the way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama lacks taste'—but not in the sense that you meant it."

[4] "Master Gotama is one without enjoyment." "There is, brahmin, a way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama is without enjoyment.' For, brahmin, those enjoyments of forms, enjoyments of sounds, enjoyments of odours, enjoyments of flavours, enjoyments of tangibles—these have been abandoned by the Tathāgata, cut off at the root, made like a palm stump, done away with, so that they are not subject to future arising. This, brahmin, is the way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama is without enjoyment'—but not in the sense that you meant it."

[5] "Master Gotama is one who teaches non-doing." "There is, brahmin, a way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama teaches non-doing.' For I, brahmin, teach the non-doing of bodily misconduct, verbal misconduct, and mental misconduct. I teach the non-doing of the manifold kinds of evil, unwholesome states. This, brahmin, is the way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama teaches non-doing'—but not in the sense that you meant it."

[6] "Master Gotama is one who teaches annihilation." "There is, brahmin, a way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama teaches annihilation.' For I, brahmin, teach the annihilation of lust, hatred, and delusion. I teach the annihilation of the manifold kinds of evil, unwholesome states. This, brahmin, is the way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama teaches annihilation'—but not in the sense that you meant it."

[7] "Master Gotama is one who is disgusted." "There is, brahmin, a way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama is one who is disgusted.' For I, brahmin, am disgusted by bodily misconduct, verbal misconduct, and mental misconduct. I am disgusted by the attaining of the manifold kinds of evil, unwholesome states. This, brahmin, is the way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama is one who is disgusted'—but not in the sense that you meant it."

[8] "Master Gotama is one who does away with things." "There is, brahmin, a way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama is one who does away with things.' For I, brahmin, teach the Dhamma for the doing away with lust, hatred, and delusion. I teach the Dhamma for the doing away with the manifold kinds of evil, unwholesome states. This, brahmin, is the way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama is one who does away with things'—but not in the sense that you meant it."

[9] "Master Gotama is a mortifier." "There is, brahmin, a way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama is a mortifier.' I say, brahmin, that the things to be burnt away are the evil, unwholesome states: bodily misconduct, verbal misconduct, mental misconduct. He, brahmin, in whom the evil, unwholesome states that are to be burnt away have been abandoned, cut off at the root, made like a palm stump, done away with, so that they are not subject to future arising—him I call a mortifier. In the Tathāgata, brahmin, the evil, unwholesome states that are to be burnt away have been abandoned, cut off at the root, made like a palm stump, done away with, so that they are not subject to future arising. This, brahmin, is the way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama is a mortifier'—but not in the sense that you meant it."

[10] "Master Gotama is one who has escaped the womb." "There is, brahmin, a way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama is one who has escaped the womb.' He, brahmin, for whom future lying in a womb, the production of renewed existence, has been abandoned, cut off at the root, made like a palm stump, done away with, so that it is not subject to future arising—him I call one who has escaped the womb. For the Tathāgata, brahmin, future lying in a womb, the production of renewed existence, has been abandoned, cut off at the root, made like a palm stump, done away with, so that it is not subject to future arising. This, brahmin, is the way in which one speaking rightly of me might say: 'The ascetic Gotama is one who has escaped the womb'—but not in the sense that you meant it."

[11] "Suppose, brahmin, there were a hen with eight or ten or twelve eggs, which she had properly covered, properly incubated, properly brooded. Whichever of those chicks should first, with the point of its claw or with its beak, break open the eggshell and safely hatch out—what should it be called: the eldest or the youngest?" "It should be called the eldest, Master Gotama, for it is the eldest of them." "Just so, brahmin, amidst a generation sunk in ignorance, become as if an egg, wholly enclosed, I alone in the world broke open the shell of ignorance and awakened to the unsurpassed perfect enlightenment. I, brahmin, am the eldest, the highest in the world. My energy, brahmin, was aroused and unflagging; my mindfulness was established and unconfused; my body was tranquil and undisturbed; my mind was concentrated and one-pointed. So I, brahmin, quite secluded from sensual pleasures, secluded from unwholesome states, entered and dwelt in the first jhāna, which is accompanied by applied and sustained thought, with rapture and pleasure born of seclusion. With the stilling of applied and sustained thought, I entered and dwelt in the second jhāna, which has internal confidence and unification of mind, is without applied and sustained thought, and has rapture and pleasure born of concentration. With the fading away of rapture, I dwelt equanimous, mindful and clearly comprehending, and I experienced pleasure with the body; I entered and dwelt in the third jhāna, of which the noble ones declare: 'Equanimous and mindful, he dwells happily.' With the abandoning of pleasure and the abandoning of pain, and with the previous disappearance of joy and grief, I entered and dwelt in the fourth jhāna, which is neither painful nor pleasant and has purity of mindfulness due to equanimity.

[12] "When my mind was thus concentrated, purified, cleansed, unblemished, free from defilements, malleable, workable, steady, and attained to imperturbability, I directed my mind to the knowledge of the recollection of past lives. I recollected manifold past lives, that is: one birth, two births, three births, four births, five births, ten births, twenty births, thirty births, forty births, fifty births, a hundred births, a thousand births, a hundred thousand births, many aeons of world-contraction, many aeons of world-expansion, many aeons of world-contraction and expansion: 'There I was so named, of such a clan, with such an appearance, such was my food, such my experience of pleasure and pain, such my life-span; passing away from there, I arose elsewhere; and there too I was so named, of such a clan, with such an appearance, such was my food, such my experience of pleasure and pain, such my life-span; passing away from there, I was reborn here.' Thus with their aspects and particulars I recollected manifold past lives. This, brahmin, was the first knowledge attained by me in the first watch of the night; ignorance was dispelled, knowledge arose; darkness was dispelled, light arose—as happens to one who dwells diligent, ardent, and resolute. This, brahmin, was my first breaking forth, like a chick's from the eggshell.

[13] "When my mind was thus concentrated, purified, cleansed, unblemished, free from defilements, malleable, workable, steady, and attained to imperturbability, I directed my mind to the knowledge of the passing away and rebirth of beings. With the divine eye, purified and surpassing the human, I saw beings passing away and being reborn, inferior and superior, beautiful and ugly, fortunate and unfortunate, and I understood how beings fare according to their kamma: 'These beings, indeed, who engaged in bodily misconduct, verbal misconduct, and mental misconduct, who reviled the noble ones, held wrong views and undertook actions based on wrong view—with the breaking up of the body, after death, they have been reborn in a state of misery, a bad destination, the lower world, hell.
