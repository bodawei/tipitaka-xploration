Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, verses 84-103

[84] At that time the Buddha, the Blessed One, was dwelling at Rājagaha on Mount Gijjhakūṭa. Now at that time a number of monks who were friends and companions, having built grass huts on the slope of Isigili, entered upon the rains-residence. The venerable Dhaniya the potter's son also built a grass hut and entered upon the rains-residence. Then those monks, when the rains-residence was over, at the end of the three months pulled down their grass huts, put away the grass and the wood, and set out on a walking tour through the countryside. But the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son stayed on in that very place for the rains, there for the winter, and there for the summer. Then, while the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son had entered the village for alms, grass-gatherers and wood-gatherers pulled down his grass hut and went off with the grass and the wood. A second time the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son collected grass and wood and made a grass hut. A second time, while the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son had entered the village for alms, grass-gatherers and wood-gatherers pulled down his grass hut and went off with the grass and the wood. A third time the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son collected grass and wood and made a grass hut. A third time, while the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son had entered the village for alms, grass-gatherers and wood-gatherers pulled down his grass hut and went off with the grass and the wood. Then it occurred to the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son: "Three times now, while I had entered the village for alms, grass-gatherers and wood-gatherers have pulled down my grass hut and gone off with the grass and the wood. Now I am well trained, accomplished and thoroughly skilled in my own hereditary craft of pottery. What if I were myself to knead clay and make a hut made entirely of clay?" Then the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son himself kneaded clay, made a hut made entirely of clay, gathered grass and wood and cow-dung, and baked that hut. That hut was beautiful, lovely and pleasing, red in colour like an indagopaka insect. And the sound of that hut was just like the sound of a little bell.

[85] Then the Blessed One, coming down from Mount Gijjhakūṭa together with a number of monks, saw that hut, beautiful, lovely, pleasing and red. Seeing it, he addressed the monks: "Monks, what is that, beautiful, lovely, pleasing and red like an indagopaka insect?" Then those monks reported the matter to the Blessed One. The Buddha, the Blessed One, rebuked him: "Monks, it is not fitting for that foolish man, not suitable, not proper, unworthy of a recluse, not allowable, not to be done. How could that foolish man make a hut entirely of clay! Surely that foolish man will have had no sympathy, no compassion, no harmlessness towards living beings! Go, monks, and break up that hut. Let not a later generation fall into destroying living beings. And, monks, a hut made entirely of clay is not to be made. Whoever should make one, there is an offence of wrong-doing." "Yes, venerable sir," those monks replied to the Blessed One, and they went to that hut; having gone, they broke up that hut. Then the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son said to those monks: "Friends, why are you breaking up my hut?" "The Blessed One, friend, is having it broken up." "Break it up, friends, if the Lord of the Dhamma is having it broken up."

[86] Then it occurred to the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son: "Three times, while I had entered the village for alms, grass-gatherers and wood-gatherers pulled down my grass hut and went off with the grass and the wood. And the hut made entirely of clay which I made has also been broken up by the Blessed One. Now I have an acquaintance who is the keeper of the timber-yard. What if I were to ask the keeper of the timber-yard for timber and make a wooden hut?" Then the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son approached the keeper of the timber-yard; having approached, he said to the keeper of the timber-yard: "Friend, three times, while I had entered the village for alms, grass-gatherers and wood-gatherers pulled down my grass hut and went off with the grass and the wood. And the hut made entirely of clay which I made has also been broken up by the Blessed One. Give me timber, friend; I wish to make a wooden hut." "Venerable sir, there is no such timber that I could give to the master. There is, venerable sir, the king's timber, set aside for repairs to the city in case of emergency. If the king gives that timber, have it carried away, venerable sir." "It has been given, friend, by the king." Then it occurred to the keeper of the timber-yard: "These recluses, sons of the Sakyans, are practisers of Dhamma, of even conduct, leading the holy life, speakers of truth, virtuous, of lovely character. Even the king has full confidence in them. It is not fitting that he should say that what was not given had been given." So the keeper of the timber-yard said to the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son: "Have it carried away, venerable sir." Then the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son had that timber cut into pieces, had it carted away, and made a wooden hut.

[87] Then the brahmin Vassakāra, chief minister of Magadha, while inspecting the works in Rājagaha, approached the keeper of the timber-yard; having approached, he said to the keeper of the timber-yard: "I say, where is that king's timber which was set aside for repairs to the city in case of emergency?" "Sir, that timber was given by His Majesty to the master Dhaniya the potter's son." Then the brahmin Vassakāra, chief minister of Magadha, was displeased: "How can His Majesty give to Dhaniya the potter's son the king's timber that was set aside for repairs to the city in case of emergency!" Then the brahmin Vassakāra, chief minister of Magadha, approached King Seniya Bimbisāra of Magadha; having approached, he said to King Seniya Bimbisāra of Magadha: "Is it true, Your Majesty, that the king's timber which was set aside for repairs to the city in case of emergency was given by Your Majesty to Dhaniya the potter's son?" "Who said so?" "The keeper of the timber-yard, Your Majesty." "Well then, brahmin, have the keeper of the timber-yard summoned." Then the brahmin Vassakāra, chief minister of Magadha, gave orders for the keeper of the timber-yard to be bound. The venerable Dhaniya the potter's son saw the keeper of the timber-yard being led away bound. Seeing him, he said to the keeper of the timber-yard: "Friend, why are you being led away bound?" "Because of the matter of that timber, venerable sir." "Go, friend; I too will come." "Come, venerable sir, before I am executed."

[88] Then the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son approached the residence of King Seniya Bimbisāra of Magadha; having approached, he sat down on a seat made ready. Then King Seniya Bimbisāra of Magadha approached the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son; having approached and paid homage to the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son, he sat down at one side. Seated at one side, King Seniya Bimbisāra of Magadha said to the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son: "Is it true, venerable sir, that the king's timber set aside for repairs to the city in case of emergency was given by me to the master?" "Yes, great king." "Venerable sir, we kings have many affairs and much to do; even having given, we might not remember. Please, venerable sir, remind me." "Do you remember, great king, that at your first consecration you spoke these words: 'Let recluses and brahmins make use of grass, wood and water — they are given'?" "I remember, venerable sir. There are, venerable sir, recluses and brahmins who are conscientious, scrupulous, desirous of training; even over a trifle scruples arise in them. It was with reference to them that I spoke, and that concerning what is in the forest and unowned. And you, venerable sir, think by that pretext to carry off timber that was not given! How could one like me strike, or bind, or banish a recluse or a brahmin dwelling in my realm! Go, venerable sir; you are freed by your hair. Do not do such a thing again." People looked down upon it, criticised and complained: "These recluses, sons of the Sakyans, are shameless, immoral, liars. Yet they will claim to be practisers of Dhamma, of even conduct, leading the holy life, speakers of truth, virtuous, of lovely character! There is no recluseship in them, there is no brahminhood in them. Their recluseship is destroyed, their brahminhood is destroyed. Where is their recluseship, where is their brahminhood! They have fallen away from recluseship, they have fallen away from brahminhood. They deceive even a king — what then of other people!" Monks heard those people looking down upon it, criticising and complaining. Those monks who were of few wishes, contented, conscientious, scrupulous, desirous of training, looked down upon it, criticised and complained: "How can the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son take the king's timber which was not given!" Then those monks, having rebuked the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son in many ways, reported the matter to the Blessed One. Then the Blessed One, on this occasion, in this connection, had the Order of monks convened and questioned the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son: "Is it true, Dhaniya, as is said, that you took the king's timber which was not given?" "It is true, Blessed One." The Buddha, the Blessed One, rebuked him: "It is not fitting, foolish man, not suitable, not proper, unworthy of a recluse, not allowable, not to be done. How could you, foolish man, take the king's timber which was not given! This, foolish man, is not for the confidence of those without confidence, nor for the increase of those with confidence; rather, foolish man, it is for the lack of confidence of those without confidence and for the wavering of some of those with confidence." Now at that time a certain monk who had formerly been a judicial minister was sitting not far from the Blessed One. Then the Blessed One said to that monk: "Monk, for how much does King Seniya Bimbisāra of Magadha, having seized a thief, have him executed, or bound, or banished?" "For a pāda, Blessed One, or for the worth of a pāda." Now at that time in Rājagaha a pāda was five māsakas. Then the Blessed One, having rebuked the venerable Dhaniya the potter's son in many ways, having spoken on being difficult to support ... and thus, monks, you should recite this training rule:

[89] "Whatever monk should take by way of theft what is not given, such that in that taking of what is not given kings, having seized a thief, would execute him, or bind him, or banish him, saying, 'You are a thief, you are a fool, you are deluded, you are a robber' — a monk taking in such a way what is not given is also one who is defeated, he is not in communion." Thus was this training rule laid down by the Blessed One for the monks.

[90] Now at that time the group of six monks went to the dyers' drying-place, stole a dyers' bundle, brought it to the monastery and divided it up. Monks spoke thus: "You are of great merit, friends. Much robe-material has accrued to you." "Where is our merit, friends? Just now we went to the dyers' drying-place and stole a dyers' bundle." "But, friends, has not a training rule been laid down by the Blessed One? Why did you, friends, steal a dyers' bundle?" "It is true, friends, that a training rule has been laid down by the Blessed One — but that concerns a village, not the forest." "But, friends, is it not just the same? It is not fitting, friends, not suitable, not proper, unworthy of a recluse, not allowable, not to be done. How could you, friends, steal a dyers' bundle! This, friends, is not for the confidence of those without confidence, nor for the increase of those with confidence; rather, friends, it is for the lack of confidence of those without confidence and for the wavering of some of those with confidence." Then those monks, having rebuked the group of six monks in many ways, reported the matter to the Blessed One. Then the Blessed One, on this occasion, in this connection, had the Order of monks convened and questioned the group of six monks: "Is it true, monks, as is said, that you went to the dyers' drying-place and stole a dyers' bundle?" "It is true, Blessed One." The Buddha, the Blessed One, rebuked them: "It is not fitting, foolish men, not suitable, not proper, unworthy of a recluse, not allowable, not to be done. How could you, foolish men, steal a dyers' bundle! This, foolish men, is not for the confidence of those without confidence, nor for the increase of those with confidence; rather, foolish men, it is for the lack of confidence of those without confidence and for the wavering of some of those with confidence." Then the Blessed One, having rebuked the group of six monks in many ways, having spoken on being difficult to support ... having praised the arousing of energy, having given the monks a talk on Dhamma befitting and suitable to that matter, addressed the monks ... "And thus, monks, you should recite this training rule:

[91] "Whatever monk should take by way of theft, from a village or from the forest, what is not given, such that in that taking of what is not given kings, having seized a thief, would execute him, or bind him, or banish him, saying, 'You are a thief, you are a fool, you are deluded, you are a robber' — a monk taking in such a way what is not given is also one who is defeated, he is not in communion."

[92] "Whatever" means: whoever, of whatever sort ... "Monk" means ... this is the sense of "monk" intended here. "Village" means: even a village of one hut is a village, a village of two huts is a village, a village of three huts is a village, a village of four huts is a village, a village with people is a village, a village without people is a village, an enclosed village is a village, an unenclosed village is a village, a settlement of the cattle-pen type is a village; and even a caravan that has settled for more than four months is called a village. "Village precincts" means: for an enclosed village, a stone's throw of a man of average build standing at the gate-post; for an unenclosed village, a stone's throw of a man of average build standing at the precincts of the house. "Forest" means: setting aside the village and the village precincts, all the rest is called forest. "What is not given" means: whatever is not given, not relinquished, not surrendered, guarded, protected, held as "mine," possessed by another. This is called "what is not given." "By way of theft" means: with a mind to steal, with a mind to take away. "Should take" means: he should take, carry off, take away, disturb its position, remove it from its place, cause it to pass an appointed spot. "Such that" means: a pāda, or the worth of a pāda, or more than a pāda. "Kings" means: a king of the earth, a regional king, district rulers, lords of intermediate territories, judges, chief ministers, or those who govern by inflicting cutting and breaking. These are called "kings." "Thief" means: whoever takes by way of theft what is not given and is worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas. He is called a "thief." "Would execute" means: they would strike with the hand, or with the foot, or with a whip, or with a cane, or with a half-staff, or would put to death by cutting. "Would bind" means: they would bind with a rope-binding, or a fetter-binding, or a chain-binding, or confinement to a house, or confinement to a city, or confinement to a village, or confinement to a market town, or would place him under a man's guard. "Would banish" means: they would banish him from a village, or from a market town, or from a city, or from a district, or from a region. "You are a thief, you are a fool, you are deluded, you are a robber" — this is abuse. "In such a way" means: a pāda, or the worth of a pāda, or more than a pāda. "Taking" means: taking, carrying off, taking away, disturbing its position, removing it from its place, causing it to pass an appointed spot. "Also he" is said with reference to the preceding case. "Is one who is defeated" means: just as a withered leaf freed from its stalk is incapable of becoming green again, so a monk, having taken by way of theft what is not given to the value of a pāda, or the worth of a pāda, or more than a pāda, is not a recluse, not a son of the Sakyans. Therefore it is said, "he is one who is defeated." "He is not in communion" means: communion is a single act, a single recitation, an equal training. This is called "communion." He has no such thing with them. Therefore it is said, "he is not in communion."

[93] What is placed on the ground, placed on a surface, placed in the air, placed aloft, placed in water, placed in a boat, placed in a vehicle, a burden, what is in a park, what is in a dwelling, what is in a field, what is on a site, what is in a village, what is in the forest; water, tooth-wood, a forest tree, goods being carried, a deposit, evasion of customs duty, a living being, the footless, the two-footed, the four-footed, the many-footed; the scout, the keeper of goods, theft by arrangement, the making of an appointment, the making of a sign.

[94] "Goods placed on the ground" means: goods deposited in the ground, buried, concealed. Thinking, "I will take away goods placed on the ground," with a mind to steal he seeks a companion, or seeks a spade or a basket, or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He cuts wood or a creeper growing there: an offence of wrong-doing. He digs, or heaps up, or removes the earth there: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches the pot: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He removes it from its place: an offence involving defeat. Having inserted his own vessel, with a mind to steal he touches what is worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He gets it into his own vessel, or takes a fistful: an offence involving defeat. With a mind to steal he touches goods strung on a thread — a necklace, or a neck-string, or a waist-string, or a cloth, or a turban: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. Taking it by the end, he lifts it: a grave offence. He draws it out by rubbing: a grave offence. He gets it free of the mouth of the pot even by the breadth of a hair's tip: an offence involving defeat. With a mind to steal he drinks at one effort ghee, or oil, or honey, or molasses worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence involving defeat. If he breaks it, or throws it away, or burns it, or makes it unusable on the spot: an offence of wrong-doing.

[95] "Goods placed on a surface" means: goods deposited on the ground-surface. Thinking, "I will take away goods placed on a surface," with a mind to steal he seeks a companion or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches it: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He removes it from its place: an offence involving defeat.

[96] "Goods placed in the air" means: goods that have gone into the air — a peacock, or a partridge, or a francolin, or a quail; or a cloth, or a turban, or money, or gold falling as it is cut loose. Thinking, "I will take away goods placed in the air," with a mind to steal he seeks a companion or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He cuts off its flight: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches it: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He removes it from its place: an offence involving defeat.

[97] "Goods placed aloft" means: goods that have been put up above — hung on a bed, or a chair, or a robe-pole, or a robe-line, or a peg in the wall, or an ivory peg, or a tree, or even on a bowl-stand. Thinking, "I will take away goods placed aloft," with a mind to steal he seeks a companion or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches it: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He removes it from its place: an offence involving defeat.

[98] "Goods placed in water" means: goods deposited in water. Thinking, "I will take away goods placed in water," with a mind to steal he seeks a companion or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He dives in or comes up: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches it: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He removes it from its place: an offence involving defeat. With a mind to steal he touches a blue lotus, or a lotus, or a white lotus, or a lotus-root, or a fish, or a tortoise growing or living there, worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He removes it from its place: an offence involving defeat.

[99] "Boat" means: that by which one crosses over. "Goods placed in a boat" means: goods deposited in a boat. Thinking, "I will take away goods placed in a boat," with a mind to steal he seeks a companion or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches it: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He removes it from its place: an offence involving defeat. Thinking, "I will take away the boat," with a mind to steal he seeks a companion or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches it: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He looses the mooring: an offence of wrong-doing. Having loosed the mooring, he touches it: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He shifts it upstream or downstream or across, even by the breadth of a hair's tip: an offence involving defeat.

[100] "Vehicle" means: a palanquin, a chariot, a cart, a wagon. "Goods placed in a vehicle" means: goods deposited in a vehicle. Thinking, "I will take away goods placed in a vehicle," with a mind to steal he seeks a companion or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches it: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He removes it from its place: an offence involving defeat. Thinking, "I will take away the vehicle," with a mind to steal he seeks a companion or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches it: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He removes it from its place: an offence involving defeat.

[101] "Burden" means: a head-load, a shoulder-load, a hip-load, something hanging down. With a mind to steal he touches a load on the head: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He lowers it to his shoulder: an offence involving defeat. With a mind to steal he touches a load on the shoulder: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He lowers it to his hip: an offence involving defeat. With a mind to steal he touches a load on the hip: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He takes it in his hand: an offence involving defeat. With a mind to steal he puts down on the ground a load that is in his hand: an offence involving defeat. With a mind to steal he takes it up from the ground: an offence involving defeat.

[102] "Park" means: a flower-park, a fruit-park. "Goods in a park" means: goods deposited in a park in any of the four positions — placed on the ground, placed on a surface, placed in the air, placed aloft. Thinking, "I will take away goods in a park," with a mind to steal he seeks a companion or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches it: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He removes it from its place: an offence involving defeat. With a mind to steal he touches a root, or bark, or a leaf, or a flower, or a fruit growing there, worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He removes it from its place: an offence involving defeat. He lays claim to the park: an offence of wrong-doing. He arouses doubt in the owner: a grave offence. When the owner, thinking, "It will not be mine," gives up his claim: an offence involving defeat. If, going to law, he defeats the owner: an offence involving defeat. If, going to law, he is defeated: a grave offence.

[103] "Goods in a dwelling" means: goods deposited in a dwelling in any of the four positions — placed on the ground, placed on a surface, placed in the air, placed aloft. Thinking, "I will take away goods in a dwelling," with a mind to steal he seeks a companion or goes there: an offence of wrong-doing. He touches it: an offence of wrong-doing. He makes it move: a grave offence. He removes it from its place: an offence involving defeat. He lays claim to the dwelling: an offence of wrong-doing. He arouses doubt in the owner: a grave offence. When the owner, thinking, "It will not be mine," gives up his claim: an offence involving defeat. If, going to law, he defeats the owner: an offence involving defeat. If, going to law, he is defeated: a grave offence.
