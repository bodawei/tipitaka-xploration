Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, verses 107-116

[107] "Jungle" means: whatever is possessed by human beings, that is jungle. "Goods resting in the jungle" means: goods deposited in the jungle in one of four positions — resting on the ground, resting on a surface, standing in the open air, suspended aloft. If, with thievish mind, thinking "I will steal goods resting in the jungle," he seeks a companion or goes there, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he touches it, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it quiver, it is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, it is an offence entailing defeat. If, with thievish mind, he touches wood or a creeper or grass growing there worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it quiver, it is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, it is an offence entailing defeat.

[108] "Water" means: water in a vessel, or in a lotus-pond, or in a reservoir. If, with thievish mind, he touches it, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it quiver, it is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, it is an offence entailing defeat. If, having lowered his own vessel into it, with thievish mind he touches water worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it quiver, it is a grave offence. If he gets it into his own vessel, it is an offence entailing defeat. If he breaks the embankment, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If, having broken the embankment, he lets out water worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, it is an offence entailing defeat. If he lets out water worth more than one māsaka but less than five māsakas, it is a grave offence. If he lets out water worth one māsaka or less than one māsaka, it is an offence of wrong-doing.

[109] "Tooth-wood" means: cut or uncut. If, with thievish mind, he touches what is worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it quiver, it is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, it is an offence entailing defeat.

[110] "A forest tree" means: a tree that is possessed by human beings and is in use. If, with thievish mind, he cuts it, then at each stroke there is an offence of wrong-doing. When one stroke remains to be given, it is a grave offence. When that stroke has been given, it is an offence entailing defeat.

[111] "Portable property" means: another's goods that can be carried off. If, with thievish mind, he touches it, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it quiver, it is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, it is an offence entailing defeat. If, thinking "I will lead away on foot the carrier together with the goods," he moves the first foot, it is a grave offence; if he moves the second foot, it is an offence entailing defeat. If, thinking "I will take the goods when they have fallen," he makes them fall, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If, with thievish mind, he touches the fallen goods worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes them quiver, it is a grave offence. If he shifts them from their place, it is an offence entailing defeat.

[112] "Goods on deposit" means: goods laid down in trust. When told, "Give me my goods," if he says, "I am not holding them," it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he raises doubt in the owner, it is a grave offence. If the owner gives up his claim, thinking, "He will not give them to me," it is an offence entailing defeat. If, going to law, he defeats the owner, it is an offence entailing defeat. If, going to law, he is defeated, it is a grave offence.

[113] "A toll barrier" means: a place established by the king in a mountain pass or at a river ford or at a village gate, with the words: "Let toll be taken from whoever enters here." If, having entered there, with thievish mind he touches goods liable to the king's duty worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes them quiver, it is a grave offence. If he takes the first foot past the toll barrier, it is a grave offence; if he takes the second foot past, it is an offence entailing defeat. If, standing within the toll barrier, he throws the goods outside the toll barrier, it is an offence entailing defeat. If he evades the toll, it is an offence of wrong-doing.

[114] "A living being" means: a human being is meant. If, with thievish mind, he touches him, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes him quiver, it is a grave offence. If he shifts him from his place, it is an offence entailing defeat. If, thinking "I will lead him away on foot," he moves the first foot, it is a grave offence; if he moves the second foot, it is an offence entailing defeat. "Footless creatures" means: snakes, fish. If, with thievish mind, he touches one worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it quiver, it is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, it is an offence entailing defeat.

[115] "Two-footed creatures" means: human beings, birds. If, with thievish mind, he touches one, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it quiver, it is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, it is an offence entailing defeat. If, thinking "I will lead it away on foot," he moves the first foot, it is a grave offence; if he moves the second foot, it is an offence entailing defeat.

[116] "Four-footed creatures" means: elephants, horses, camels, oxen, donkeys, small livestock. If, with thievish mind, he touches one, it is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it quiver, it is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, it is an offence entailing defeat. If, thinking "I will lead it away on foot," he moves the first foot, it is a grave offence; if he moves the second foot, it is a grave offence; if he moves the third foot, it is a grave offence; if he moves the fourth foot, it is an offence entailing defeat.
