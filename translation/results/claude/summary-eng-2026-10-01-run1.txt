Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, section verses 180-207 (condensed prose summary)

When bhikkhus out of compassion praised the beauty of death to a sick bhikkhu who then died, the Buddha declared them guilty of an offence entailing defeat. A series of further cases of accidental death followed. A bhikkhu on almsround sat down on a stool and crushed a child hidden under a cloth: no offence entailing defeat, but henceforth one must examine a seat before sitting, on pain of an offence of wrong-doing. Deaths caused by a falling pestle, a rolled mortar-block, and similar mishaps were ruled no offence, being unintentional. A son who pushed his father, a bhikkhu, without intending death incurred no offence; with the intention of causing death and the father dying, he incurred defeat; if the father did not die, a grave offence. The same threefold pattern was applied to a bhikkhu who struck the throat of another choking on meat, causing him to bring up bloody flesh; to a bhikkhu who unknowingly served poisoned almsfood (no offence, since he did not know) as against one who gave poison intending to make a test (a grave offence); and to the Āḷavaka bhikkhus engaged in building work, where stones, bricks, adzes and rafters dropped from above, scaffolding was fastened, roofs were descended from, and trees were felled on another's word — in each case unintentional or without intent to kill meaning no offence, intent plus death meaning defeat, intent without death meaning a grave offence.

A bhikkhu oppressed by discontent threw himself from Gijjhakūṭa and crushed a basket-maker: no defeat, but one must not throw oneself down, on pain of an offence of wrong-doing; likewise the group of six who hurled a rock in sport and killed a cowherd were forbidden to throw stones in play. Medical treatments of the sick — sweating, nose-treatment, massage, bathing, anointing with oil, raising up, laying down, giving food or drink — likewise incurred no offence without murderous intent, defeat if death followed intent, a grave offence if it did not.

Several cases of abortion were ruled offences entailing defeat: a bhikkhu who supplied an abortifacient to an adulterous wife; one who did so for a barren co-wife jealous of the fertile wife, the child dying; and those who told a woman to crush or heat her womb. Where the mother died but not the child, or both or neither died, the offence was a grave one. Giving medicine to make a barren woman conceive, or a fertile woman barren, with fatal result, was an offence of wrong-doing only. When the group of six tickled a bhikkhu of the group of seventeen till he died of breathlessness, and when the seventeen smothered one of the six, there was no defeat. A bhikkhu versed in spirits who deprived a yakkha of life incurred a grave offence. Sending a bhikkhu to a haunt of fierce yakkhas, through wilderness infested with beasts, or through bandit country, with or without murderous intent, followed the same threefold scheme. Killing one person while meaning another, or in any combination of intention and object, was defeat. Striking a bhikkhu possessed by a non-human being, speaking of heaven to a doer of good deeds who died enraptured, or of hell to an evil-doer who died terrified, and the group of six setting fire to the jungle, were judged by the same criterion of intent. A bhikkhu who told an executioner to dispatch a condemned man with a single stroke incurred defeat; if the executioner refused and killed in his own way, an offence of wrong-doing. A bhikkhu who told relatives of a man whose hands and feet had been cut off to give him buttermilk, and a bhikkhunī who in a like case advised salted sour gruel, both incurred defeat.

While the Blessed One was dwelling at Vesālī in the Great Wood in the Hall with the Peaked Roof, many friendly bhikkhus spent the rains on the bank of the Vaggumudā river during a famine in the Vajjian land, when food was gained only by tickets and life was hard. Debating how to pass the rains in concord and without want, they rejected taking up work for householders and carrying their messages, and chose instead to praise one another's superhuman states to the laity — that such a bhikkhu gained the first, second, third or fourth jhāna, that such a one was a stream-enterer, once-returner, non-returner, arahant, master of the three knowledges or the six direct knowledges. The people, delighted at having such virtuous bhikkhus, gave them food and drink better than they used for their own parents, children, servants, friends and kinsmen, and the bhikkhus grew handsome and fresh-faced, while bhikkhus returning from other regions were thin, wretched and pale. When the Buddha, as is the custom of Tathāgatas, asked how they had passed the rains, they told him, admitting the claims were untrue. He rebuked them as foolish men: better that their bellies be cut open with a sharp butcher's knife than that for the belly's sake they should praise one another's superhuman states, for the former brings only death or deadly pain, the latter rebirth in hell. He then described the five great thieves in the world: the robber chief who roams villages, towns and royal cities with a hundred or a thousand followers plundering and killing, and like him the evil bhikkhu who craves to roam about honoured and revered by householders and renunciants and to gain robes, almsfood, lodging and medicines; the evil bhikkhu who learns the Dhamma and Discipline taught by the Tathāgata and claims it as his own; the one who groundlessly accuses a pure celibate of unchastity; the one who uses the Saṅgha's heavy property — monastery and its site, dwelling and its site, bed, chair, mattress, bolster, metal pots and vessels, adze, axe, hatchet, hoe, chisel, creepers, bamboo, reeds, grass, clay, wooden and earthen goods — to court and win over laypeople. But the chief great thief in the world with its devas, Māras and Brahmās, its recluses, brahmins, gods and humans, is he who boasts of a superhuman state that is not real and not present in him; for such a one has eaten the country's almsfood by theft.

The rule was then laid down: whatever bhikkhu, without direct knowledge, should claim a superhuman state of himself, knowledge and vision worthy of the noble ones, saying "thus I know, thus I see," and afterwards, whether cross-questioned or not, having fallen and looking for purification, should say, "Friend, not knowing I said I know, not seeing I said I see; I spoke what was empty and false" — he too is one defeated, not in communion. Because many bhikkhus who perceived as seen, attained, reached and realized what was not so declared final knowledge through overestimation, and later found their minds inclining to lust, hate and delusion, Ānanda reported their remorse, and the Buddha said such a declaration through overestimation is not to be reckoned as an offence, adding the exemption "except through overestimation."

The analysis explains the terms: "without direct knowledge" means not knowing or seeing in oneself a wholesome state that is absent, unreal, non-existent, yet saying "I have it." A superhuman state is jhāna, liberation, concentration, attainment, knowledge and vision, development of the path, realization of fruit, abandoning of defilements, the mind's freedom from hindrances, delight in an empty hut. "Of himself" means applying those states to oneself or oneself to them; knowledge means the three knowledges; declaring means announcing to a woman or man, householder or renunciant. "Afterwards" means when that moment has passed; "being cross-questioned" means being asked what was attained, how, when, where, which defilements were abandoned, which states gained; "having fallen" means having boasted falsely of a superhuman state from evil desire and so fallen into defeat; "looking for purification" means wishing to become a householder, a lay follower, a monastery attendant or a novice. As a palm tree cut off at the crown cannot grow again, so such a bhikkhu is no recluse, no son of the Sakyan; and communion — one formal act, one recitation, an equal training — is not his.

There follows the exhaustive permutation. Jhāna means the four jhānas; liberation, concentration and attainment each the empty, signless and undirected; knowledge and vision the three knowledges; development of the path the four foundations of mindfulness, four right efforts, four bases of success, five faculties, five powers, seven factors of enlightenment and the noble eightfold path; realization of fruit the four fruits up to arahantship; abandoning of defilements the abandoning of lust, hate and delusion; freedom of mind from these; and delight in an empty hut by each of the four jhānas. For one deliberately lying "I attained the first jhāna," there is an offence entailing defeat by three factors — before speaking he knows he will lie, while speaking he knows he lies, after speaking he knows he lied — and by four, five, six or seven factors with the addition of misrepresenting his view, his acceptance, his preference, his state. The same applies to "I attain," "I have attained," "I am a gainer of," "I am a master of," "it has been realized by me," and so likewise for the second, third and fourth jhānas, for each liberation, concentration and attainment, for the three knowledges, for each set of the path-factors, for each of the four fruits, for claims that lust, hate or delusion has been cast off, vomited out, released, abandoned, relinquished, expelled and wholly expelled, and for claims that the mind is freed from lust, hate or delusion — in every case deliberate lying entails an offence entailing defeat.
