Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, section verses 132-161 (condensed prose summary)

At that time the group-of-six bhikkhus went to a dyers' drying-ground and carried off the dyers' bundle; questioned, the Buddha declared they had incurred an offence entailing defeat. In similar cases, a bhikkhu who merely conceived a thievish mind on seeing a costly cloth incurred nothing; one who touched it with thievish mind incurred an offence of wrong-doing; one who made it move, a grave offence; one who shifted it from its place, defeat. The same applied to an almsman who saw a costly upper covering. A bhikkhu who marked goods by day intending to steal by night incurred defeat whether he took the thing he meant or another; but if he mistakenly carried off his own property, only wrong-doing. Likewise a bhikkhu stealing another's load incurred defeat at whatever stage—head, shoulder, hip, hand, ground—he thievishly shifted it.

Where a bhikkhu, having put away another's robe or bowl lest it be lost, said "I took it," the Buddha ruled there is no offence in a mere manner of speaking; the same was ruled for a bhikkhunī. One who picked up a cloth blown by a whirlwind to return it to its owners was guiltless, having no thievish mind; one who seized a turban "before the owners see" incurred defeat. A bhikkhu took a rag from an undecayed corpse inhabited by a departed spirit, which pursued him; there was no defeat, but henceforth taking rags from an undecayed corpse is wrong-doing. Shifting the lot-marker to get a better robe at a distribution was defeat. Ānanda, wearing another's inner robe thinking it his own, was guiltless as perceiving it his own. Bhikkhus eating the leavings of lions, tigers, leopards, hyenas and wolves committed no offence, such things being owned by an animal. Falsely claiming an absent monk's share of rice, food, cakes, sugarcane or fruit was not defeat but an offence of expiation for deliberate lying, whereas actually stealing a bowlful of rice, meat or cakes in time of famine was defeat.

Many further cases follow. Taking a bag together with its stool, stealing the Saṅgha's mattress or a robe from the robe-line, or stealing within a dwelling and not going out, all entail defeat. Taking a friend's share on trust, or eating another's portion thinking it one's own, is no offence. Goods dropped by thieves—mangoes, rose-apples, breadfruit, jackfruit, palm-fruit, sugarcane, timbarūsaka—eaten while perceiving them as rags from a dust-heap involve no offence, but eating them with thievish mind "before the owners see" is defeat; so too with stray raft-timbers on the Aciravatī, a herdsman's cloth, washermen's cloth caught on the foot, and meat dropped by a kite. Saying "tell them I said so" to obtain goods from another's supporters is wrong-doing. Carrying a gem past a toll-station knowingly is defeat; unknowingly, no offence. Releasing a trapped pig, deer or fish out of compassion is blameless; doing so thievishly is defeat. Theft by arrangement makes all the conspirators guilty of defeat, even if each share is under five māsakas.

Taking the Saṅgha's water, clay, heaped grass, bed, stool, mattress, pillow, door, window or rafter thievishly is defeat; burning heaped grass is wrong-doing; borrowing wood temporarily is no offence. Visiting bhikkhus consuming Saṅgha fruit for use, and accepting fruit from the keepers appointed over it, are blameless. Furniture belonging to one dwelling may not be used elsewhere, though temporary removal of uposatha seating is allowed. Thullanandā's pupils, obtaining food by falsely invoking their teacher's wish, incurred expiation for deliberate lying. Ajjuka, who pointed out a hidden treasure to the nephew whom the householder had designated as faithful, was declared blameless by Upāli, against Ānanda's charge. Pilindavaccha, who restored kidnapped children by psychic power, was blameless in the range of his power. Paṇḍuka, who thought himself already no recluse and so engaged in sexual intercourse, incurred defeat not for theft but for sexual intercourse. Finally, Daḷhika's pupil, who had stolen a turban worth less than five māsakas, was told there was no defeat; instructed in Dhamma, he took delight again in the life.
