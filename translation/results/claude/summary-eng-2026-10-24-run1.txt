Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, section verses 162-179 (condensed prose summary)

While the Buddha was staying at Vesālī in the Great Wood at the Hall with the Peaked Roof, he spoke repeatedly in praise of the unattractive, of the development of the perception of the unattractive, and of the attainment based on the unattractive, and then announced that he wished to go into seclusion for a fortnight and was to be approached by none but the one who brought his almsfood. The bhikkhus, applying themselves in many ways to the development of the perception of the unattractive, came to be troubled, ashamed and disgusted with their own bodies, as a young person fond of adornment would be disgusted by the carcass of a snake, dog or human hung about the neck. So they took their own lives, took one another's lives, and went to Migalaṇḍika the sham recluse saying, "Good sir, deprive us of life; this bowl and robe shall be yours." Rewarded with bowls and robes, he killed many bhikkhus. Washing his bloodied sword at the river Vaggumudā, remorse arose in him at having slain virtuous bhikkhus; but a deity of Māra's company came over the unbroken water and praised him for "ferrying across those not yet crossed." Elated, he went from dwelling to dwelling with a sharp sword asking, "Who is not crossed? Whom shall I ferry across?" Bhikkhus still with lust felt fear and terror; those free of lust did not. He killed one, two, three, up to sixty bhikkhus in a single day.

When the fortnight ended, the Buddha emerged and asked Ānanda why the Saṅgha seemed so diminished. Ānanda recounted what had happened and asked him to teach another method whereby the Saṅgha might be established in final knowledge. The Buddha had all the bhikkhus living near Vesālī assembled and taught concentration through mindfulness of breathing: peaceful, sublime, unadulterated, a pleasant abiding which makes evil unwholesome states vanish and subside on the spot, as a great unseasonable rain-cloud settles the dust stirred up in the last month of the hot season. He described the practice: a bhikkhu goes to the forest, the root of a tree or an empty hut, sits cross-legged with body erect and mindfulness established before him, breathes in and out mindfully, knowing long breaths as long and short as short, and trains in experiencing the whole body, in calming the bodily formation, in experiencing rapture, pleasure, the mental formation and its calming, in experiencing the mind, gladdening, concentrating and liberating the mind, and in contemplating impermanence, dispassion, cessation and relinquishment.

The Buddha then convened the Saṅgha, questioned the bhikkhus, confirmed the facts, and rebuked the deed as unseemly, unbefitting a recluse, improper and not to be done, and laid down the rule: whatever bhikkhu intentionally deprives a human being of life or seeks out a knife-bringer for him is one who incurs an offence entailing defeat and is not in communion.

Later, a certain lay follower was ill, and his wife was beautiful. The group of six bhikkhus, infatuated with her, reasoned that if he lived they would not obtain her, and so praised the beauty of death to him: "You have done good and no evil; what use is this wretched, miserable life? Death is better for you; you will be reborn in a heavenly world and enjoy the five divine strands of sense pleasure." Convinced, he ate, chewed, tasted and drank unsuitable things, fell gravely ill and died. His wife and other people complained that these shameless Sakyan recluses, liars claiming to be virtuous and truthful, had killed her husband by praising death. Modest bhikkhus reported this, and the Buddha rebuked those foolish men and amended the rule: whatever bhikkhu intentionally deprives a human being of life, or seeks out a knife-bringer for him, or praises the beauty of death, or incites him to death, saying "Good man, what use is this wretched, miserable life? Death is better for you than life"—thus intending, thus purposing in mind, and in various ways praises the beauty of death or incites to death—he too incurs an offence entailing defeat and is not in communion.

The word-analysis follows. "Intentionally" means knowing, conscious, deliberate transgression. "Human being" means from the first consciousness arising in the mother's womb until the time of death. "Deprives of life" means cuts off the life-faculty, destroys the continuity. A knife-bringer may be a sword, spear, javelin, club, stone, knife, poison or rope. "Praises the beauty of death" means showing the danger in life and extolling death; "incites to death" means saying "Take a knife, eat poison, hang yourself with a rope." A wretched life is explained by comparison—the poor compared with the rich, humans compared with devas—and a miserable life as that of one whose hands, feet, ears or nose have been cut off. "Thus intending" means perceiving death, intending death, aiming at death. One who thus kills becomes no recluse, no son of the Sakyan, like a great stone split in two that cannot be joined again; and communion—a single formal act, a single recitation, an equal training—is not his.

The modes of killing are then enumerated: in person, by command, by a messenger, by a succession of messengers, by a messenger who goes astray, by a messenger who comes back; speaking out openly or in private, with or without correct perception; praising death by body, by speech, by both, by messenger, by writing; and by pit-trap, leaning-support, placing a weapon nearby, medicine, and the presenting of forms, sounds, smells, tastes, tangibles and mental objects; by telling, by instructing, by making an appointed sign, by making a signal.

In the command cases: ordering a bhikkhu to kill a named person is an offence of wrong-doing; if the killer kills the intended person, both incur defeat; if, thinking it is the one named, he kills another, the original instigator is not guilty and only the killer incurs defeat; if, thinking it another, he nonetheless kills the one named, both incur defeat; if he kills another thinking it another, only the killer incurs defeat. With a chain of messengers, each transmission is an offence of wrong-doing; the instigator incurs a grave offence when the killer accepts the commission, and all incur defeat when the killing is done; but if the commission is passed to a different person than directed, the original instigator is not guilty, while the one who gave that order and the killer incur defeat. If the messenger returns unable to do it and is ordered to do it when he can, both incur defeat on the killing. If the instigator feels remorse but does not send word "Do not kill," both incur defeat; if he sends word and the other kills anyway, saying "I was commanded by you," only the killer incurs defeat; if the other desists, neither is guilty. Merely exclaiming "Oh, that so-and-so were dead!" is an offence of wrong-doing in every combination of privacy and perception.

For praising death by body, speech, both, messenger or writing—"one who dies thus gains wealth, fame, or goes to heaven"—there is an offence of wrong-doing (letter by letter in the case of writing); if painful feeling arises in the victim through that praise, a grave offence; if he dies, defeat. Digging a pit for a particular person carries the same graded penalties; if dug without specifying anyone, there is wrong-doing at the digging, wrong-doing when a human falls in, a grave offence at the painful feeling, and defeat at death; if a yakkha, peta or non-human in human form falls in and dies, the final penalty is a grave offence; if an animal falls in and dies, an offence of expiation. The same gradation applies to rigging a leaning-support with a knife, poison, weakening, or over a pit or precipice; to placing a weapon, poison or rope within reach; to giving ghee, butter, oil, honey or molasses intending death; to presenting frightful or delightful forms, sounds, smells, tastes and touches (the latter intending that the victim pine away through not obtaining them); to speaking of hell to one destined for hell so as to terrify him, or of heaven to a doer of good so that he dies intent upon it; to telling when asked and instructing when unasked that "one who dies thus gains wealth, fame or heaven." Making an appointed sign—before or after a meal, by night or day—or making a signal by winking, raising an eyebrow or lifting the head, is an offence of wrong-doing; if the killing is done at that sign, both incur defeat, but if done before or after the appointed sign, the original instigator is not guilty and only the killer incurs defeat.

There is no offence for one who acts unintentionally, who does not know, who had no intention of causing death, who is mad, or for the first offender.
