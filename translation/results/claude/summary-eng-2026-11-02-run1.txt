Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, section verses 180-192 (condensed prose summary)

At Sāvatthi and elsewhere various incidents arose concerning the third pārājika on depriving a human being of life, and the Buddha determined each case. Bhikkhus who out of compassion praised the beauty of death to a sick bhikkhu, so that he died, had committed an offence entailing defeat. But a bhikkhu who unknowingly sat on a child covered by a cloth and crushed it was not guilty, though the Buddha laid down an offence of wrong-doing for sitting on a seat without first examining it. A pestle that fell and killed a child, and a mortar-block that rolled over one, brought no offence, being unintentional.

A repeated pattern then governs many cases: where there was no intention of causing death, there is no offence; where there was the intention of causing death and death followed, the offence entails defeat; where death did not follow, it is a grave offence. Thus with a son who pushed his father so that he fell and died; with a bhikkhu who struck the throat of another choking on meat, dislodging it with blood; with bhikkhus at Āḷavī raising stones, bricks, adzes and rafters who let them fall on a bhikkhu below, or directed another to stand in a dangerous place while tying scaffolding, climbing down from a roof, or felling a tree; with those who steamed, gave nasal treatment, massaged, bathed, anointed with oil, made to rise or lie down, or gave food or drink to a sick bhikkhu; with striking a bhikkhu seized by a non-human being; with speaking of heaven to a doer of good deeds so that he died longing for it, or of hell to an evil-doer so that he died of terror; with setting fire to a forest; and with sending a bhikkhu into a dwelling of fierce yakkhas, a wilderness of beasts of prey, or a wilderness of robbers. Where the fatal stone, brick, adze or rafter slipped from a careless grip, there was no offence, being unintentional.

A bhikkhu who unknowingly gave poisoned almsfood to others who died was not guilty, not knowing; one who gave poison intending to make a test incurred a grave offence. A bhikkhu oppressed by discontent who threw himself from Gijjhakūṭa and crushed a basket-maker to death was not guilty of defeat, but the Buddha forbade throwing oneself down, an offence of wrong-doing; likewise the group of six, who hurled a stone in sport and killed a cowherd, incurred an offence of wrong-doing, and hurling stones in sport was forbidden.

In matters of abortion, a bhikkhu who frequented a family and supplied an abortifacient to a woman pregnant by a lover, or to a co-wife's rival at a barren wife's request, incurred defeat when the child died; if only the mother died, or both died, or neither, it was a grave offence. Telling a woman to crush or to heat the womb, whereby the foetus was aborted, entailed defeat. Giving medicine so that a barren woman might conceive, or that a fertile woman might not, when the woman died, was an offence of wrong-doing only.

The group of six tickled a bhikkhu of the group of seventeen with their fingers until he died of breathlessness, and the seventeen smothered one of the six while meaning to do a formal act against him: in neither case was there an offence entailing defeat. A bhikkhu versed in spirits who deprived a yakkha of life incurred a grave offence. Killing the intended person, or another by mistake in any combination of perception, entails defeat.

A bhikkhu who told an executioner to dispatch a condemned man with a single blow, and was obeyed, incurred defeat; if the executioner refused and killed in his own way, only an offence of wrong-doing. Finally, a bhikkhu who advised relatives of a man whose hands and feet had been cut off to give him buttermilk, and a bhikkhunī who similarly advised salted sour gruel, so that the man died, each incurred an offence entailing defeat.
