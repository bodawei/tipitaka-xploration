Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, section verses 193-207 (condensed prose summary)

At Vesālī, in the Great Wood at the Hall with the Peaked Roof, the Blessed One was dwelling when a number of bhikkhūs, friends and companions, took up the rains residence on the bank of the river Vaggumudā. At that time the Vajjī country was famine-stricken, with food scarce, the crops blighted, and food distributed by ticket, so that one could not keep alive by gleaning alms. The bhikkhūs considered how they might pass the rains in concord, without quarrelling, and not be troubled over alms-food. Some proposed undertaking work for the householders, others proposed carrying their messages; but others said: "Enough of such things. Let us rather praise one another's superhuman states to the laity — 'such a bhikkhu has attained the first jhāna, such the second, the third, the fourth; such a one is a stream-enterer, a once-returner, a non-returner, an arahant, a master of the three true knowledges, one of the six direct knowledges.' Then they will think to give to us." This course seemed best, and they did so. The people rejoiced, saying, "What a gain for us that such bhikkhūs — virtuous and of lovely character — have come to spend the rains with us," and they gave the bhikkhūs food, delicacies and drink finer than they consumed themselves or gave to their parents, wives and children, servants, friends or kinsfolk. So those bhikkhūs became handsome, with faculties well nourished, bright of countenance and clear of complexion.

Now it is the custom of bhikkhūs at the end of the rains to go and see the Blessed One. After the three months these bhikkhūs put their lodgings in order, took bowl and robe, and travelled to Vesālī, to the Great Wood, and approached the Blessed One, saluting him and sitting to one side. Bhikkhūs who had spent the rains in other regions were thin, wretched, of poor colour, yellowed, their limbs showing the veins; but those from the Vaggumudā bank were handsome and bright. As is the custom of the Buddhas, the Blessed One exchanged greetings with the newcomers, asking whether they had been able to endure, whether they had lived the rains in concord without being troubled over alms-food. They answered that all had been well. Though Tathāgatas ask knowing, and knowing do not ask, they question bhikkhūs for two reasons: in order to teach the Dhamma, or in order to lay down a training rule for the disciples. So the Blessed One asked how they had managed so comfortably, and they related what had happened. "But was it true of you, bhikkhūs?" "It was not true, Blessed One."

The Buddha rebuked them: "Unbecoming, foolish men, unseemly, improper, unworthy of a recluse, not allowable, not to be done. How could you, for the sake of your bellies, praise one another's superhuman states to householders? Better that your belly were cut open with a sharp butcher's knife than that you should do so. For by the former cause you would incur death or deadly pain, but not on that account pass after death to a state of woe, the bad destination, the abyss, hell; whereas by this you would. This is not for the faith of the faithless." Having rebuked them and given a talk on Dhamma, he addressed the bhikkhūs on the five kinds of great thief found in the world. First is the thief who thinks, "When shall I roam the villages, towns and capitals surrounded by a hundred or a thousand, killing and having others kill, cutting and having others cut, burning and having others burn?" — and in time does so; just so an evil bhikkhu thinks, "When shall I wander surrounded by a hundred or a thousand, honoured, revered, esteemed by householders and those gone forth, a getter of robes, alms-food, lodgings and medicinal requisites?" — and in time does so. Second is the evil bhikkhu who, having mastered the Dhamma and Discipline proclaimed by the Tathāgata, gives it out as his own. Third is the evil bhikkhu who charges a pure celibate living the pure holy life with a groundless accusation of unchastity. Fourth is the evil bhikkhu who wins over and fawns upon householders with the heavy property and heavy requisites of the Saṅgha — monastery and monastery site, dwelling and dwelling site, bed, bench, mattress, pillow, metal pots, vessels, jars and cauldrons, adzes, axes, hatchets, spades, chisels, creeper, bamboo, muñja and pabbaja grass, grass, clay, wooden goods and earthenware. But in this world with its devas, Māras and Brahmās, among this generation with its recluses and brahmins, its devas and humans, the foremost great thief is he who boasts of a superhuman state that is not there and not true; for such a one has eaten the country's alms-food by way of theft.

Having rebuked them in many ways and spoken of being hard to support and hard to maintain, the Blessed One laid down the rule: whatever bhikkhu, without direct knowledge, should claim of himself a superhuman state, knowledge and vision worthy of the noble ones, saying "thus I know, thus I see," and afterwards, whether being cross-questioned or not, having fallen and looking for purification, should say, "Friends, not knowing I said I know, not seeing I said I see; I spoke what was empty and false" — he too is one entailing defeat, not in communion.

Later a number of bhikkhūs who perceived as seen what was not seen, as attained what was not attained, as reached what was not reached, as realised what was not realised, declared final knowledge through overestimation; but afterwards their minds inclined to lust, hate and delusion, and they were remorseful, wondering whether they had fallen into an offence entailing defeat. They told the venerable Ānanda, who told the Blessed One. "There are, Ānanda, bhikkhūs who declare final knowledge through overestimation; but that is not to be reckoned as an offence." So the rule was amended with the exception "except through overestimation."

The analysis follows. "Without direct knowledge" means not knowing or seeing in oneself a wholesome state that is absent, untrue, non-existent, yet saying "I have that wholesome state." A superhuman state means jhāna, liberation, concentration, attainment, knowledge and vision, development of the path, realisation of the fruits, abandoning of the defilements, the mind's being unobstructed, delight in an empty hut. "Of himself" means he applies those wholesome states to himself or himself to them. Knowledge means the three true knowledges; vision is that same knowledge. "Should claim" means should announce it to a woman or a man, a householder or one gone forth. "Afterwards" means when that moment, that instant, that second has passed. "Being cross-questioned" means being examined on the matter admitted: "What have you attained? In what way? When? Where? Which defilements have you abandoned? Of which states are you a getter?" "Not being cross-questioned" means spoken to by nobody. "Having fallen" means that, being of evil desires and overcome by desire, he has boasted of an untrue superhuman state and fallen into an offence entailing defeat. "Looking for purification" means wishing to become a householder, a lay follower, a monastery attendant or a novice. Just as a palm tree with its crown cut off cannot grow again, so a bhikkhu of evil desires, overcome by desire, who boasts of an untrue superhuman state, is no recluse, no son of the Sakyan: hence "one entailing defeat." Communion means a single formal act, a single recitation, an equal training; he has none of this with them: hence "not in communion."

The superhuman states are then enumerated in detail: the four jhānas; the empty, signless and undirected liberations, concentrations and attainments; knowledge and vision as the three true knowledges; development of the path as the four foundations of mindfulness, four right strivings, four bases of psychic power, five faculties, five powers, seven factors of enlightenment and the noble eightfold path; realisation of the fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning and arahantship; abandoning of lust, hatred and delusion; the mind's being unobstructed by lust, hatred and delusion; and delight in an empty hut through each of the four jhānas.

Then the permutations are set out. If one speaks a deliberate lie saying "I attained the first jhāna," there is an offence entailing defeat by three factors — beforehand he knows he will lie, while speaking he knows he is lying, afterwards he knows he has lied; by four factors, with these and misrepresentation of his view; by five, adding misrepresentation of his belief; by six, adding misrepresentation of his preference; by seven, adding misrepresentation of his state. The same holds for the forms "I attain," "I have attained," "I am a getter of the first jhāna," "I am a master of the first jhāna," "the first jhāna has been realised by me." As the first jhāna is expanded, so all the rest are to be expanded in each of these forms and by three up to seven factors: the second, third and fourth jhānas; the empty, signless and undirected liberations, concentrations and attainments; the three true knowledges; the four foundations of mindfulness, right strivings and bases of psychic power; the five faculties and powers; the seven factors of enlightenment; the noble eightfold path; the fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning and arahantship. Likewise, to say by three up to seven such factors, "Lust — or hatred, or delusion — has been relinquished, vomited, released, abandoned, given up, cast off, utterly cast off by me," or "My mind is unobstructed by lust, by hatred, by delusion," speaking a deliberate lie, is an offence entailing defeat.
