Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, section verses 132-161 (condensed prose summary)

At a washermen's ground the group-of-six monks carried off some washing and worried whether they had fallen into offence; the Buddha declared them fallen into offence entailing defeat. A monk who merely formed a thievish thought about an expensive cloth was exempt, but one who touched it incurred an offence of wrong-doing, one who made it shake a grave offence, and one who shifted it from its place offence entailing defeat. The same sequence was repeated for an almsman and a stored article. A monk who marked goods by day intending theft at night, and stole that or another or a different thing in each combination of expectation, was fallen; but one who mistakenly took his own goods was exempt from defeat, incurring only wrong-doing. A monk who, carrying another's goods, touched a head-load, made it shake, lowered it from shoulder to hip, took it in hand, or set down and lifted it with thievish mind, was fallen. When a monk left a robe in the open and another stored it away, the owner's angry charge that he was "no recluse" was resolved by the exemption for a mere manner of speaking (niruttipatha); the same held for a bowl, and for a nuns' robe case. A monk who caught a cloak whirled up by the wind to return it to its owners was exempt; one who caught it with thievish mind was fallen; likewise a wind-blown turban. A monk taking rag-cloth from an unbroken corpse was followed home by the corpse's inhabiting spirit; the Buddha ruled it no offence entailing defeat but forbade taking rag-cloth from an unbroken corpse. A monk who moved a boundary-marker to take a robe during distribution was fallen. Venerable Ānanda, thinking another's under-robe his own, put it on: no offence for one perceiving it as his own. Monks who cooked and ate leavings of lion, tiger, leopard, hyena and wolf kills were exempt, since such are owned by animals. A monk who falsely claimed another's share of rice-porridge, solid food, cake, sugar-cane or jujube was exempt from defeat but incurred expiation for deliberate lying. In a famine, monks who stole bowlfuls of rice, meat, cakes, sweetmeats or sesame-balls were fallen; and the marking-and-stealing combinations were repeated, with wrong-doing where one took his own. A monk who moved a whole bench to take a bag, saying "taking from here I shall be fallen," was fallen; so for a couch, a robe on a robe-rail, and a robe stolen in a dwelling that he resolved not to leave. A friend who took and consumed another's share on trust was exempt. When shares were laid out during robe-making, a monk who took another's portion either through thinking it his own or because it was set down in his own bowl was exempt. Monks who accepted fruit dropped by thieves and consumed it perceiving it as rag from a dust-heap were exempt; the same with mango, rose-apple, jack-fruit, palm-fruit, sugar-cane and jujube thieves, and so on for each kind, but those who ate the dropped goods with thievish mind before the owners saw them were fallen. Monks who stole the Sangha's mango, rose-apple, jack-fruit, palm-fruit, sugar-cane or jujube were fallen, as were those who stole picked flowers or flowers they picked, worth five months' provisions. A monk sent as a messenger to a family who then had a robe fetched for himself was exempt from defeat but the Buddha laid down that one should not say "I am sent, let me go" — doing so is wrong-doing; so too if both parties used that wording. A man travelling with a monk hid a jewel in the monk's bag to pass the customs unnoticed: the unknowing monk was exempt. When a man feigned illness, gave his goods to a monk and, after passing the toll-station, asked for them back, the unknowing monk was exempt. But a monk who, enticed with goods, carried a jewel through the toll-station was fallen. A monk who out of compassion freed a pig caught in a snare was exempt; one who freed it with thievish mind before the owners saw it was fallen; likewise for a deer and for fish caught in a fish-trap. A monk who, seeing goods in a cart and thinking "taking from here I shall be fallen," took them by sideways sliding was fallen. A monk who took a piece of meat dropped by a hawk intending to return it was exempt; one who took it with thievish mind was fallen. When a raft's bindings broke and the logs scattered, monks who drew them out perceiving them as rag were exempt; those who did so with thievish mind were fallen. A cowherd's cloak, hung on a tree while he relieved himself, was taken by a monk perceiving it as rag: exempt. When a washermen's cloth floating free stuck to a monk's foot as he crossed a river, he was exempt if he took it to return it, fallen if with thievish mind. A monk who ate bit by bit from a jar of ghee was exempt from defeat but incurred wrong-doing. Several monks made a pact to steal; one did the theft while the others claimed they were not fallen, but the Buddha declared them all fallen. When several stole goods together and divided them, each share not filling five months' provision, they too were all fallen. In a famine at Sāvatthī, monks who stole handfuls of rice, mung beans, black gram or sesame from a shop were fallen. In the Blind-Wood at Sāvatthī, monks who took and ate the remains of a cow, and then of a pig, killed by thieves, perceiving them as rag, were exempt. Monks who stole cut or self-cut grass worth five months' provisions were fallen. Visiting monks who had the Sangha's fruits distributed and ate them were charged by resident monks but were exempt, acting for the purpose of use; and men who guarded mangoes, rose-apples, jack-fruits, palm-fruits, sugar-cane and jujubes and gave them to monks—the cautious monks who refused, fearing the givers had no right to give, were told there is no offence in a watchman's gift. A monk who took the Sangha's timber temporarily and propped his own dwelling wall was exempt for a temporary borrowing. A monk who stole the Sangha's water, clay, or heaped grass was fallen; one who burned heaped grass with thievish mind was exempt from defeat but incurred wrong-doing. Monks who stole the Sangha's couch, bench, bedding, pillow, door, window-opening or door-bolt were fallen. When monks used one layman's dwelling-only furniture elsewhere, he complained and the Buddha forbade using dwelling-only property elsewhere, making it wrong-doing. Monks, scrupulous about moving furniture, sat on the ground for the recitation and other affairs and soiled their limbs and robes; the Buddha allowed moving it temporarily. At Campā a nun who had a drink cooked for another nun, and one at Rājagaha who had honey-cakes cooked, then ate them herself, were each exempt from defeat but incurred expiation for deliberate lying. At Vesālī the householder Ajjuka's nephew and son were tested; Ajjuka told the faithful one of them of the source, and he used his wealth to establish his family and give alms; the son complained to Ānanda that Ajjuka had told their inheritance to "one with a mother," and called him no recluse. Upāli, on Ajjuka's side, asked whether one told by an owner to tell a named person of a source incurs anything; Ānanda said nothing, not even wrong-doing, and Upāli concluded Ajjuka was exempt. At Bārāṇasī, robbers raided Pilindavaccha's supporting families and took two children; by psychic power he brought them back and lodged them in the palace, and though monks complained, the Buddha declared no offence for one with psychic power within its range. Two friends, Paṇḍuka and Kapila, lived apart; one crossing a river caught a piece of fat from a pig-butcher's hand, and when a cowherd girl invited him to sexual intercourse he did so, thinking himself "by nature no recluse"; the Buddha ruled no offence entailing defeat for taking what was not given, but offence entailing defeat for engaging in sexual intercourse. At Sāgala the monk Daḷhika's pupil, oppressed by discontent, stole a shopkeeper's turban and announced he would leave the Order; Daḷhika had the value calculated, found it not worth five months' provisions, declared it no offence entailing defeat, and gave a teaching on the matter, and the monk delighted in it.
