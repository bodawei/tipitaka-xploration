Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, section verses 162-179 (condensed prose summary)

At Vesālī, in the Great Wood at the Hall with the Peaked Roof, the Buddha praised in many ways the meditation on the unattractive and the attainment based on it. Wishing to meditate in seclusion for half a month, he instructed the monks that no one should approach him except the one who brought his almsfood. The monks, taking up the practice of contemplating the foulness of the body, grew so disgusted with their own bodies—as a young man or woman would be repelled by a snake's, a dog's, or a human corpse hung about the neck—that they killed themselves and one another, and urged the sham recluse Migalaṇḍikā, a knife-bringer, to kill them in exchange for their bowls and robes.

Having slaughtered many monks, Migalaṇḍikā washed his bloodstained sword in the Vaggumudā river and was seized with remorse at the evil he had done. But a Māra-spirit, walking on the water, urged him on, saying that he had done great merit in ferrying across those not yet crossed. Taking this as encouragement, he went from dwelling to dwelling crying, "Who is unferried? Whom shall I ferry?" and killed one, two, up to sixty monks in a single day, the passionless among them feeling no fear. Emerging from seclusion, the Buddha saw the diminished community, and Ānanda explained what had happened, asking for another teaching by which the Saṅgha might find firm footing.

The Buddha then taught concentration through mindfulness of breathing: cultivated and made much of, it is peaceful, excellent, unadulterated, a pleasant abiding, and it makes arisen evil unwholesome states vanish and subside, as a great unseasonal rain-cloud lays the summer dust. He detailed the sixteen steps—mindful breathing long and short, experiencing the whole body, calming the bodily formation, experiencing rapture, pleasure, and the mental formation, calming the mental formation, experiencing and gladdening and concentrating and liberating the mind, and contemplating impermanence, dispassion, cessation, and relinquishment.

He then questioned the monks and rebuked them for killing themselves and one another and for praising the beauty of death, and laid down the training rule: a monk who intentionally deprives a human being of life, or seeks a knife-bringer for him, or praises the beauty of death, or incites him to die, saying "What use is this wretched life to you? Death is better than life," with mind and intent set on death, inciting him in various ways—such a one is one who has committed an offence entailing defeat, not in communion.

Later the group-of-six monks, enamoured of a sick layman's beautiful wife, praised death to him, telling him his good deeds were done and that after death he would be reborn in heaven and enjoy the five divine pleasures. The layman, convinced, ate and drank what was unsuitable and died of a severe illness. His wife and others upbraided the monks, and the monks reported it. The Buddha rebuked the group-of-six and extended the rule to cover praising the beauty of death and inciting to die.

The Buddha then defined the terms: "intentionally" means knowing, aware, deliberate, and transgressing; a "human being" means from the first thought's arising in the womb until the time of death; to "deprive of life" means to cut off the life-faculty; a "knife-bringer" includes a sword, spear, arrow, club, stone, knife, poison, or rope. Praising death means showing the faults of life and the virtues of death, and inciting to die means telling someone to fetch a knife, eat poison, or hang himself. "Offence entailing defeat" is like a great stone split in two that can never be rejoined—the monk is no longer a recluse, and "not in communion" means he no longer shares the common formal act, recitation, and training.

The Buddha then analysed the modes of killing one by one: killing by one's own hand, by command, by messenger, by a messenger who passes the commission on, by a messenger who goes astray, by one who returns after delivering the message, and the cases of acting openly while thinking it secret, secretly while thinking it open, and both; praising by body, speech, or both; praising by messenger or by letter; and the various means—pit-trap, leaning-support, placing a weapon nearby, giving medicine or unsuitable food, presenting frightening or pleasing sights, sounds, smells, tastes, tangibles, or teachings, telling when asked, instructing unasked, and making appointed signs or signals. In each case he specified the offence: an offence of wrong-doing for the preliminary step, a grave offence when the victim feels pain and does not die, and defeat when the victim dies. In the cases of mistaken identity, the original instigator is exempt and only the killer incurs defeat; where the killer repents or desists, both are exempt. There is no offence when unintentional, unknowing, without intent to cause death, for a deranged monk, or for the first offender.
