Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, section verses 180-192 (condensed prose summary)

The incident that prompted the third pārājika arose when some bhikkhus, out of compassion for a sick companion, praised the beauty of death to him; he died, and the Buddha declared that they had incurred an offence entailing defeat. A series of further cases follows, distinguishing intent. A bhikkhu who sat down without inspecting a seat and crushed a child hidden under a cloth was not guilty of a pārājika but was made subject to a rule of wrong-doing for failing to check a seat; a bhikkhu who accidentally knocked over a pestle or water-vessel onto a child, and builders at Āḷavī who dropped stones, bricks, chisels or rafters on those below without intending death, were likewise exempt as unintentional. Where, however, a bhikkhu pushed a fellow monk with the intention of causing death — a son shoving his father at the Sangha's summons, a blow to the throat on a choking man, a stone or brick or rafter loosed from above, a hand at the saw — the outcome depended on the result: if death followed, it was a pārājika; if not, a grave offence. Reckless acts — throwing oneself from the Vulture's Peak in discontent, or the group-of-six hurling stones for sport in playfulness, killing a cowherd — brought only a wrong-doing, with new rules against jumping from a height and against throwing stones for sport. Other cases establish that a bhikkhu unaware he was carrying poisoned alms-bread, and one who gave poison merely intending to make a test, did not commit a pārājika (the tester incurred a grave offence). The same pattern is applied to medical treatment: sweating, nasal medicine, massage, bathing, anointing with oil, raising up or laying down a sick man, and giving him food or drink were no offence when done without intent to kill, but a pārājika when death was intended and resulted, and a grave offence when it did not. The case of a monk who killed a yakkha by exorcism brought a grave offence. In abortion cases, a bhikkhu who supplied an abortifacient on request from a monk's regular household, or advised a pregnant woman to massage or heat herself so as to miscarry, was defeated if the child or both died; if only the mother died it was a grave offence, and if neither died, likewise a grave offence. Giving medicine to a barren woman to make her conceive, or to a fertile woman to prevent conception, resulted in a wrong-doing when she died. The group-of-six who tickled a seventeen-group bhikkhu to death with finger-pokes, and the seventeen-group who crushed a group-of-six bhikkhu in fun during a formal act, were exempt. Sending a fellow monk into a haunt of fierce yakkhas, a wilderness of wild beasts, or a wilderness of thieves intending his death gave a pārājika if he was killed and a grave offence if not; the same held for a monk seized by a non-human. One who, taking one person for another, killed someone, incurred a pārājika. Speaking of the heaven awaiting good deeds, or of hell to a hell-bound man who died of fright, was no offence without intent, but a pārājika with intent when death followed, and a grave offence otherwise. Cutting a tree at Āḷavī so that it fell on a monk gave a pārājika with intent, a grave offence without death; the group-of-six setting fire to a forest brought no offence without intent, but a pārājika or grave offence with it. A bhikkhu who urged an executioner to kill a criminal with a single blow so as not to torment him was defeated when the man complied on his word; but when the executioner killed the condemned man in despite of his instruction, the offence was only a wrong-doing. Finally, a bhikkhu who advised a mutilated man's grieving relatives to give him takka to drink, and a bhikkhunī who advised giving loṇa-sovīraka, with death resulting, were both declared defeated — the bhikkhunī's case being reported by the bhikkhunīs through the bhikkhus to the Buddha.
