Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, section verses 193-207 (condensed prose summary)

At Vesālī, in the Kūṭāgārasālā in the Great Wood, a group of bhikkhus who were friends and companions spent the rains retreat on the bank of the river Vaggumudā. At that time the Vajjis were afflicted by famine, and it was hard to live by gleaning and begging. The bhikkhus considered how they might dwell together comfortably through the rains without weariness from alms; some proposed doing work for laypeople, others carrying messages for them, but these were rejected. Finally they agreed to praise one another's superhuman states to the householders, saying that such and such a bhikkhu was a gainer of the first, second, third or fourth jhāna, a stream-enterer, once-returner, non-returner, arahant, one with the three true knowledges, one with the six higher knowledges, thinking the people would then give abundantly. This they did, and the householders, believing they were fortunate to have such virtuous bhikkhus, gave them the best of their food, eating and giving to their families only inferior fare. So those bhikkhus became handsome, with bright complexions and clear faculties, while the bhikkhus who had spent the rains in other districts arrived thin, haggard and sallow, their bodies covered with veins.

When the Vaggumudā bhikkhus came to pay homage to the Buddha after the retreat, he asked them whether they had fared well; they replied that they had. The Buddha, knowing the matter, asked how they had passed the rains so comfortably, and they told him everything. Asked whether it was true, they admitted it. The Buddha rebuked them: it was unbecoming, improper, not in accord with the training, not fit for recluses, and impermissible; for the sake of their bellies they had spoken of one another's superhuman states. It would be better for them to have their bellies cut open with a sharp butcher's knife than to do this; for by such a deed one might meet death or deadly pain, but not the hells, whereas by this one falls into the realm of misery and hell after death. Having rebuked them and given a Dhamma talk, the Buddha addressed the bhikkhus on the five great thieves in the world: the one who aspires to wander with a hundred or a thousand followers raiding and slaying; the wicked bhikkhu who, having learned the Dhamma and Vinaya proclaimed by the Tathāgata, appropriates it for himself; the wicked bhikkhu who falsely accuses a pure brahmacārin of unchastity; the wicked bhikkhu who takes the Saṅgha's heavy property—monasteries and lands, beds, benches, mattresses, metal vessels, tools, grass, clay, wooden and earthenware—and uses it to ingratiate himself with householders; and the greatest of all thieves, the one who boasts of a non-existent, untrue superhuman state, for by such a person the country's almsfood is eaten by way of theft.

Having rebuked them in many ways for being hard to support, the Buddha laid down the training rule: whatever bhikkhu, not having known and seen, should declare a superhuman state—a knowledge and vision worthy of the noble ones—as applying to himself, saying, "Thus I know, thus I see," and later, when cross-questioned or not cross-questioned, being guilty and wishing for purification, should say, "Friends, not knowing I said I knew; not seeing I said I saw—I spoke empty falsehood," then that also is an offence entailing defeat, not in communion. When the rule was thus laid down, many bhikkhus who through overestimation had declared final knowledge, perceiving what was unseen as seen, what was unattained as attained, later found their minds inclining to passion, aversion and delusion; anxious, they reported the matter to Ānanda, who told the Buddha. The Buddha said that such bhikkhus who so declare through overestimation are not to be reckoned as having committed an offence. He then reissued the rule with the addition "except through overestimation."

The text then defines its terms: "not having known" means not knowing or seeing in oneself any wholesome state; "superhuman state" means jhāna, liberation, concentration, attainment, knowledge and vision, development of the path, realization of the fruits, destruction of the taints, unobstructedness of mind, and delight in an empty hut. "Applying to himself" means applying those wholesome states to oneself or oneself to them; "knowledge" means the three true knowledges, and "vision" is that knowledge, and that vision is that knowledge. "Should declare" is to announce to a woman or a man, layperson or renunciant; "thus I know, thus I see" is to say one knows and sees these states, that they exist in one, and that one is found among them. "Later" is after the moment of the declaration has passed; "being cross-questioned" is being examined about the matter as to what, how, when and where one attained, and which taints were abandoned; "not cross-questioned" is not being accused by anyone. "Guilty" is having made the boast with evil desire, overcome by longing, and thus committing the offence entailing defeat. "Wishing for purification" is wishing to become a householder, lay devotee, monastery attendant or novice. The statement of having spoken empty falsehood is defined as saying one does not know or see these states, that they are not in one and one is not found among them, and that one has spoken empty, false, untrue words in ignorance. "Except through overestimation" excludes overestimation. "That also" refers back to what was said before. "Is one who has committed an offence entailing defeat" means that, like a palm tree cut off at the crown, he cannot grow again; such a bhikkhu, having boasted with evil desire of an untrue superhuman state, is no longer a true recluse, no longer a son of the Sakyan, and is therefore called one entailing defeat. "Not in communion" means he has no communion, no shared act, recitation or training, with the Saṅgha.

The rule is then applied in great detail: the offence entailing defeat accrues to one who knowingly utters falsehood with three, four, five, six or seven constituents—anticipating the lie, lying, and having lied, and with distortion of view, belief, preference and state—whether saying "I attain, I am attaining, I have attained the first jhāna," "I am a gainer of it," "I have mastery of it," or "it has been realized by me," and equally for the second, third and fourth jhānas, the empty, signless and undirected liberations, concentrations and attainments, the three true knowledges, the four foundations of mindfulness, four right strivings, four bases of psychic power, five faculties, five powers, seven factors of enlightenment, the noble eightfold path, the fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning and arahantship, and for claiming that passion, hatred or delusion has been abandoned, vomited out, released, given up, renounced and cut off, or that one's mind is unobstructed by them—each such utterance is an offence entailing defeat.
