Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ

[104] "Field" means a place where grain crops or pulse crops grow. "Goods situated in a field" means goods deposited in a field in one of four positions: resting on the ground, resting on a surface, resting in the air, resting suspended aloft. If, with intent to steal, thinking "I will take away goods situated in a field," he seeks a companion or goes there, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he touches them, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes them move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts them from their place, there is an offence involving defeat. If, with intent to steal, he touches grain crops or pulse crops grown there worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes them move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts them from their place, there is an offence involving defeat. If he lays claim to the field, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he causes doubt to arise in the owner, there is a grave offence. If the owner abandons his claim, thinking "It will not be mine," there is an offence involving defeat. If, going to law, he defeats the owner, there is an offence involving defeat. If, going to law, he is defeated, there is a grave offence. If he shifts a stake or a cord or a fence or a boundary marker, there is an offence of wrong-doing. So long as one act remains to be done, there is a grave offence. When that act has been done, there is an offence involving defeat.

[105] "Site" means the site of a monastery park, the site of a dwelling. "Goods situated on a site" means goods deposited on a site in one of four positions: resting on the ground, resting on a surface, resting in the air, resting suspended aloft. If, with intent to steal, thinking "I will take away goods situated on a site," he seeks a companion or goes there, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he touches them, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes them move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts them from their place, there is an offence involving defeat. If he lays claim to the site, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he causes doubt to arise in the owner, there is a grave offence. If the owner abandons his claim, thinking "It will not be mine," there is an offence involving defeat. If, going to law, he defeats the owner, there is an offence involving defeat. If, going to law, he is defeated, there is a grave offence. If he shifts a stake or a cord or a fence or a wall, there is an offence of wrong-doing. So long as one act remains to be done, there is a grave offence. When that act has been done, there is an offence involving defeat.

[106] "Goods situated in a village" means goods deposited in a village in one of four positions: resting on the ground, resting on a surface, resting in the air, resting suspended aloft. If, with intent to steal, thinking "I will take away goods situated in a village," he seeks a companion or goes there, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he touches them, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes them move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts them from their place, there is an offence involving defeat.

[107] "Wilderness" means that which is owned by people: that is wilderness. "Goods situated in the wilderness" means goods deposited in the wilderness in one of four positions: resting on the ground, resting on a surface, resting in the air, resting suspended aloft. If, with intent to steal, thinking "I will take away goods situated in the wilderness," he seeks a companion or goes there, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he touches them, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes them move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts them from their place, there is an offence involving defeat. If, with intent to steal, he touches wood or creepers or grass grown there worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, there is an offence involving defeat.

[108] "Water" means water that is in a vessel or in a lotus pond or in a reservoir. If, with intent to steal, he touches it, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, there is an offence involving defeat. If, having put in his own vessel, he touches with intent to steal water worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it move, there is a grave offence. If he gets it into his own vessel, there is an offence involving defeat. If he breaks the embankment, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If, having broken the embankment, he lets out water worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, there is an offence involving defeat. If he lets out water worth more than one māsaka but less than five māsakas, there is a grave offence. If he lets out water worth one māsaka or less than one māsaka, there is an offence of wrong-doing.

[109] "Tooth-stick" means one that is cut or one that is not cut. If, with intent to steal, he touches one worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, there is an offence involving defeat.

[110] "Forest tree" means a tree owned by people and used by them. If, with intent to steal, he cuts it, for each stroke there is an offence of wrong-doing. So long as one stroke remains to be given, there is a grave offence. When that stroke has been given, there is an offence involving defeat.

[111] "Portable property" means goods belonging to another that can be carried. If, with intent to steal, he touches them, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes them move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts them from their place, there is an offence involving defeat. If, thinking "I will carry off both the goods and the bearer on foot," he moves the first foot, there is a grave offence. If he moves the second foot, there is an offence involving defeat. If, thinking "I will take the goods when they have fallen," he makes them fall, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If, with intent to steal, he touches the fallen goods worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes them move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts them from their place, there is an offence involving defeat.

[112] "Deposit" means goods placed in trust. When he is told, "Give me my goods," if he says "I did not receive them," there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he causes doubt to arise in the owner, there is a grave offence. If the owner abandons his claim, thinking "He will not give it to me," there is an offence involving defeat. If, going to law, he defeats the owner, there is an offence involving defeat. If, going to law, he is defeated, there is a grave offence.

[113] "Customs boundary" means a place established by the king at a mountain pass or a river ford or a village gate, thus: "Let them take duty from anyone who enters here." If, with intent to steal, he touches goods there liable to the king's duty, worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes them move, there is a grave offence. If he moves the first foot beyond the customs boundary, there is a grave offence. If he moves the second foot beyond, there is an offence involving defeat. If, standing within the customs boundary, he throws the goods beyond the customs boundary, there is an offence involving defeat. If he evades the duty, there is an offence of wrong-doing.

[114] "Living being" means a human being is meant. If, with intent to steal, he touches him, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes him move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts him from his place, there is an offence involving defeat. If, thinking "I will lead him away on foot," he moves the first foot, there is a grave offence. If he moves the second foot, there is an offence involving defeat. "Footless creatures" means snakes and fish. If, with intent to steal, he touches one worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, there is an offence involving defeat.

[115] "Two-footed creatures" means human beings and winged creatures. If, with intent to steal, he touches one, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, there is an offence involving defeat. If, thinking "I will lead it away on foot," he moves the first foot, there is a grave offence. If he moves the second foot, there is an offence involving defeat.

[116] "Four-footed creatures" means elephants, horses, camels, cattle, donkeys, and livestock. If, with intent to steal, he touches one, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, there is an offence involving defeat. If, thinking "I will lead it away on foot," he moves the first foot, there is a grave offence. If he moves the second foot, there is a grave offence. If he moves the third foot, there is a grave offence. If he moves the fourth foot, there is an offence involving defeat.

[117] "Many-footed creatures" means scorpions, centipedes, and caterpillars. If, with intent to steal, he touches one worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, there is an offence involving defeat. If, thinking "I will lead it away on foot," he moves it, for each foot there is a grave offence. When he moves the last foot, there is an offence involving defeat.

[118] "Scout" means one who, having spied out goods, tells another: "Steal such-and-such goods" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. If the other steals those goods, there is an offence involving defeat for both. "Watchman" means one guarding goods that have been brought: if, with intent to steal, he touches goods worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes them move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts them from their place, there is an offence involving defeat. "Theft by arrangement" means that when several have made an arrangement together and one of them steals the goods, there is an offence involving defeat for all of them.

[119] "Making an appointment" means that he makes an appointment thus: "Steal those goods before the meal or after the meal, by night or by day, according to that appointment" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. If the other steals those goods according to that appointment, there is an offence involving defeat for both. If he steals those goods before or after that appointed time, there is no offence for the instigator; for the one who steals there is an offence involving defeat.

[120] "Making a sign" means that he makes a sign: "I will wink the eye, or raise the eyebrow, or lift the head; at that sign steal those goods" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. If the other steals those goods at that sign, there is an offence involving defeat for both. If he steals those goods before or after that sign, there is no offence for the instigator; for the one who steals there is an offence involving defeat.

[121] A monk commands a monk: "Steal such-and-such goods" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. If the other, recognising them as such, steals them, there is an offence involving defeat for both. A monk commands a monk: "Steal such-and-such goods" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. If the other, recognising them as such, steals something else, there is no offence for the instigator; for the one who steals there is an offence involving defeat. A monk commands a monk: "Steal such-and-such goods" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. If the other, thinking them to be something else, steals them, there is an offence involving defeat for both. A monk commands a monk: "Steal such-and-such goods" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. If the other, thinking them to be something else, steals something else, there is no offence for the instigator; for the one who steals there is an offence involving defeat. A monk commands a monk: "Tell so-and-so that so-and-so should tell so-and-so that so-and-so should steal such-and-such goods" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. If that one informs the next, there is an offence of wrong-doing. When the one who is to steal accepts the commission, there is a grave offence for the original instigator. If that one steals the goods, there is an offence involving defeat for all of them. A monk commands a monk: "Tell so-and-so that so-and-so should tell so-and-so that so-and-so should steal such-and-such goods" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. If that one commands someone else, there is an offence of wrong-doing. When the one who is to steal accepts the commission, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If that one steals the goods, there is no offence for the original instigator; for the one who passed on the command and for the one who steals there is an offence involving defeat. A monk commands a monk: "Steal such-and-such goods" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. That one goes and returns again, saying, "I am unable to steal those goods." The other commands him again: "When you are able, then steal those goods" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. If that one steals the goods, there is an offence involving defeat for both. A monk commands a monk: "Steal such-and-such goods" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. Having given the command, he feels remorse but does not make it heard: "Do not steal it." If that one steals the goods, there is an offence involving defeat for both. A monk commands a monk: "Steal such-and-such goods" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. Having given the command, he feels remorse and makes it heard: "Do not steal it." If that one, saying "I was commanded by you," steals the goods, there is no offence for the instigator; for the one who steals there is an offence involving defeat. A monk commands a monk: "Steal such-and-such goods" — there is an offence of wrong-doing. Having given the command, he feels remorse and makes it heard: "Do not steal it." If that one says "Very well" and desists, there is no offence for either.

[122] For one who takes what is not given, there is an offence involving defeat in five ways: it is possessed by another, and he perceives it as possessed by another, and the article is valuable, worth five māsakas or more than five māsakas, and the intention to steal is present. If he touches it, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it move, there is a grave offence. If he shifts it from its place, there is an offence involving defeat.

[123] For one who takes what is not given, there is a grave offence in five ways: it is possessed by another, and he perceives it as possessed by another, and the article is of little value, worth more than one māsaka but less than five māsakas, and the intention to steal is present. If he touches it, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he makes it move, there is an offence of wrong-doing. If he shifts it from its place, there is a grave offence.
