Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, section verses 43-66 (condensed prose summary)

At Vesālī a number of Vajjiputtaka monks, having eaten, slept and bathed as much as they wished, attended unwisely and engaged in sexual intercourse without renouncing the training or declaring their weakness. Later, afflicted by loss of relatives, wealth and by illness, they came to Venerable Ānanda saying they blamed neither Buddha, Dhamma nor Saṅgha, but only themselves: being unlucky and of little merit, they had failed to keep the complete and pure holy life for life, though if allowed to go forth and be ordained again under the Blessed One they would now strive night and day in the cultivation of wholesome states conducive to awakening. Ānanda reported this, but the Buddha declared it impossible that a Tathāgata should abolish an already-laid-down pārājika training rule for the sake of the Vajjians. He then declared that one who indulges in intercourse without having renounced the training and declared his weakness must not be ordained if he returns, whereas one who first renounces the training and declares his weakness may be ordained on return; and he laid down the rule: "Whatever monk, endowed with the training and mode of life shared by monks, should engage in sexual intercourse without having renounced the training and declared his weakness, even with a female animal, is one who is defeated (pārājika), not in communion."

The words are then analysed. "Whatever monk" covers any monk of any sort, name, clan or standing; among the many senses of "bhikkhu" the one intended is a monk ordained by a united Saṅgha through a formal act with a motion and three announcements, unchallengeable and fit to stand. Of the three trainings—higher virtue, higher mind, higher wisdom—the higher virtue is meant; "shared mode of life" means the training rules laid down by the Blessed One, in which one trains.

Regarding renouncing the training, a distinction is drawn between merely declaring weakness and actually renouncing. A monk who is discontented, wishing to fall away from recluseship, ashamed and disgusted with monkhood and longing for the state of a householder, lay follower, monastery attendant, novice, sectarian, sectarian's disciple, non-recluse or non-son-of-the-Sakyans, who says such things as "Suppose I were to renounce the Buddha (or Dhamma, Saṅgha, training, Vinaya, Pātimokkha, recitation, preceptor, teacher, pupil, co-resident, fellow-student, companion in the holy life)," or "Suppose I were a householder," and similar formulations with "if," "perhaps," "come now," "it occurs to me"—only declares weakness; the training is not renounced. Likewise if he says "I remember my mother, father, brother, sister, son, daughter, wife, relatives, friends, village, town, field, property, money, gold, craft, or former laughter, talk and play," or "I have a mother etc. who must be supported by me," or "who will support me," or "I have a village, field, money, craft by which I shall live," or says "It is hard, not easy, hard to practise, I cannot bear it, I do not delight in it"—all these are only declarations of weakness.

The training is actually renounced when, in that same state of discontent, he says and makes known in the present tense, "I renounce the Buddha," "the Dhamma," "the Saṅgha," "the training," "the Vinaya," "the Pātimokkha," "the recitation," "my preceptor," "teacher," "pupil," "co-resident," "fellow-student," "companion"; or "Consider me a householder, lay follower, monastery attendant, novice, sectarian, sectarian's disciple, non-recluse, non-son-of-the-Sakyans"; or "Enough of the Buddha for me," "What is the Buddha to me?", "I have no use for the Buddha," "I am well rid of the Buddha," and so on down to companions in the holy life; likewise with any synonyms for these terms, expressed by such modes, marks and signs.

The training is not renounced if one who is mad, deranged or overwhelmed by pain renounces it, or if it is renounced before such a person, or before a deity or an animal; if renounced in a language the hearer does not understand (a noble tongue to a barbarian, a barbarian tongue to one of noble speech, or either where the hearer does not comprehend); if done in jest or hastily, if he speaks without wishing to be heard or wishes to be heard but does not make it heard, if he informs one who does not understand, or fails to inform one who does, or does not make it heard at all.

"Sexual intercourse" is that unworthy, vulgar, base, lewd practice, done in private, the coupling of pairs. "Engages in" means insertion of organ into organ, even to the depth of a sesame seed. "Even with a female animal" shows that a monk who so acts is no recluse, no son of the Sakyans—all the more with a human woman. "Pārājika" is likened to a man whose head is cut off, who cannot live by rejoining it: so such a monk cannot live in the monkhood. "Not in communion" means he no longer shares the one formal act, one recitation and equal training that constitute communion.

The casuistry then enumerates three classes each of women, hermaphrodites, eunuchs and men (human, non-human, animal). With women and hermaphrodites, penetration by any of the three passages—anus, vagina, mouth—entails pārājika; with eunuchs and men, the two passages, anus and mouth. Where the monk's mind consents to indulgence and he inserts, the offence is pārājika. Where enemies—monks, kings, robbers, rogues, or those who assault with lotus-scent—bring a woman or other such person and force the monk's organ upon them, he incurs pārājika if he consents to the entering, the entered state, the remaining, or the withdrawal; if he consents to none of the four, there is no offence. The same holds whether the other is awake, asleep, drunk, mad, negligent, dead and undecomposed, or dead and mostly undecomposed; but with a corpse mostly decomposed the offence is a grave one (thullaccaya). The same schema applies where the monk himself is brought to another, and where either or both parties are covered or uncovered (covered to uncovered, uncovered to covered, both covered, both uncovered).

Inserting passage into passage, passage into non-passage, or non-passage into passage entails pārājika; non-passage into non-passage entails thullaccaya. If a monk misconducts himself with a sleeping monk or novice (or a novice with a sleeping monk or novice), and the one awakened consents, both are to be expelled; if he does not consent, only the offender is expelled.

There is no offence for one who does not know, who does not consent, for one who is mad, deranged, or overwhelmed by pain, and for the first offender.
