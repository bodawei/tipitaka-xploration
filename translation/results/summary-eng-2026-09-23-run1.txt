Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, section verses 67-131 (condensed prose summary)

A long series of cases was brought to the Buddha for judgment. A bhikkhu who had intercourse with a female monkey was declared to have incurred an offence entailing defeat, as were the Vajjiputtaka bhikkhus of Vesālī who indulged without first renouncing the training or declaring their weakness. Various stratagems devised to escape the rule — acting in lay guise, naked, or wearing garments of grass, bark, wood-shavings, human hair, animal hair, owls' wings or antelope hide — availed nothing: all incurred defeat. A bhikkhu who out of lust inserted his thumb into a girl lying on a stool, killing her, was guilty only of a saṅghādisesa offence. When a young man hid in the hut of the bhikkhunī Uppalavaṇṇā and violated her, the Buddha declared there is no offence for one who does not consent. When a sex-change occurred, the Buddha allowed the bhikkhu turned woman to join the bhikkhunīs (and vice versa) keeping the same preceptor, ordination and years, clearing offences shared in common before the other community, and being free of offences not shared.

Intercourse with mother, daughter, sister or a former wife likewise entailed defeat, as did self-penetration by a supple-backed or well-endowed bhikkhu. Using the wound beside a corpse's genitals brought defeat; touching a clay or wooden image's private part with the genital organ brought only an offence of wrong-doing. Sundara, accosted in the street by a woman who took his organ in her mouth, was innocent since he did not consent. Women's suggestions that a bhikkhu need not exert himself, or should emit outside or inside, did not exempt him. With corpses: an undecayed or mostly undecayed body entailed defeat; a mostly decayed one, a grave offence; penetrating the severed neck of a head so as to touch entailed defeat, without touching an offence of wrong-doing; using assembled bones, an offence of wrong-doing. Intercourse with a female nāga, yakkhinī, petī or a paṇḍaka entailed defeat. A bhikkhu whose faculties were numbed was still guilty whether he felt anything or not; one who stopped at mere contact incurred a saṅghādisesa offence.

Several bhikkhus resting by day at Bhaddiya, Sāvatthī's Andhavana and Vesālī's Mahāvana were mounted by women while their limbs were stiffened by wind. The Buddha explained that the organ becomes workable in five ways — through lust, excrement, urine, wind, or the bite of a caterpillar — and that lust was impossible in an arahant; such bhikkhus were blameless, and he allowed door-closing when secluding oneself by day. Those who consented to entry, insertion, remaining and withdrawal incurred defeat; those who did not know, did not consent, or threw the woman off were blameless. Upāli declared that there is no offence for what occurs in a dream. The laywomen Supabbā of Rājagaha and Saddhā of Sāvatthī, holding that giving sexual intercourse is the highest gift, induced bhikkhus to rub between thighs, navel and other places or to be handled — saṅghādisesa offences, not defeat. When Licchavi youths forced bhikkhus upon bhikkhunīs, probationers, novices, prostitutes, paṇḍakas, laywomen, or upon one another, whoever consented was to be expelled, and whoever did not consent was blameless. A bhikkhu whose former wife sat upon him when he fell backward was blameless, not consenting; but a forest-dwelling bhikkhu who consented while a fawn drinking his urine took his organ in its mouth incurred defeat.

At Rājagaha, Dhaniya the potter's son, whose grass hut was thrice dismantled by grass- and wood-gatherers, built and fired an all-clay hut, beautiful and red like an indagopaka beetle. The Buddha had it destroyed out of compassion for living beings and forbade such huts. Dhaniya then obtained the king's timber, stored for repairing the city, by falsely telling the timber-keeper that King Bimbisāra had given it. Vassakāra the Magadhan minister reported this; the timber-keeper was bound, and Dhaniya, summoned, pleaded the king's coronation words that ascetics might use grass, wood and water freely. Bimbisāra replied that this referred only to ungarnered forest goods and released him, saying "by a hair you are free." People murmured that these Sakyan ascetics deceive even a king. The Buddha rebuked Dhaniya and, learning from a former judge that the king punished theft of a pāda or more (a pāda being five māsakas at Rājagaha), laid down the rule against taking what is not given by way of theft, entailing defeat; after the group of six stole a dyer's bundle from the drying-ground, claiming the rule applied only to villages, he extended it to "from a village or from the jungle."

There follows the word-analysis: definitions of village, village precincts, jungle, "not given," "by way of theft," "should take," kings, thief, beating, binding, banishment, and defeat — one guilty being like a withered leaf fallen from its stalk, no longer able to become green, and excluded from communion, that is, from a single formal act, a single recitation, an equal training. Then the categories of theft are analysed: goods resting on the ground, on a surface, in the open air, suspended aloft, in water, on a boat, in a vehicle, carried as a load, in a park, monastery, field, site, village or jungle; water, tooth-wood, forest trees, portable property, goods on deposit, toll barriers, living beings, footless, two-footed, four-footed and many-footed creatures, scouts, warehouse-guards, theft by arrangement, appointed signs and signals. In each case, searching or approaching and touching incur an offence of wrong-doing, making the article shake a grave offence, and shifting it from its place defeat; with land and buildings, laying claim is wrong-doing, raising doubt in the owner a grave offence, and the owner's giving up his claim, or defeating him at law, defeat. For a load, lowering it from head to shoulder, shoulder to hip, hip to hand, or setting it down, is defeat. A two-footed or human being carried off by foot: defeat at the second step; a four-footed, at the fourth. Instigators and accomplices are treated at length: if the deed is done as commanded, both incur defeat; if the wrong goods or a substitute agent is used, or if the instigator recalls the order in time and is heeded, the original instigator is not guilty.

Finally the fivefold and sixfold sets: defeat when the article is owned by another and perceived as such, valuable, worth five māsakas or more, and taken with thievish mind; a grave offence if worth more than one and less than five; wrong-doing if worth a māsaka or less; likewise when one is not perceiving it as one's own, not taking on trust, not borrowing temporarily. There is no offence for one who thinks it his own, who takes on trust, takes temporarily, takes what belongs to petas or animals, thinks it a rag from a dust-heap, and for one who is mad, deranged in mind, overwhelmed by pain, or is the first offender.
