Pārājikapāḷi — 1. Pārājikakaṇḍaṃ
vin01m.mul.xml, section verses 132-179 (condensed prose summary)

A long series of cases illustrating the rule against taking what is not given was brought to the Buddha. The group of six bhikkhus went to a dyers' drying-ground and carried off a dyer's bundle: they had incurred an offence entailing defeat. Other bhikkhus who saw costly cloth there were told that the mere arising of a thievish mind is no offence, touching the goods with thievish mind is an offence of wrong-doing, making them quiver is a grave offence, and shifting them from their place is defeat. The same gradation applied to an almsman who saw a costly rug. One who marked an article by day intending to steal it at night incurred defeat whether he took the thing he meant or another, but if he mistakenly carried off his own property it was only wrong-doing. A bhikkhu carrying another's load who with thievish mind moved it from head to shoulder, shoulder to hip, hip to hand, or set it on the ground and took it up again, incurred defeat.

Where a fellow bhikkhu had merely put away a robe or bowl to keep it safe and said "I took it," the Buddha, learning that he spoke only as a manner of speaking, declared no offence; the same for a bhikkhunī. One who picked up cloth blown by a whirlwind meaning to return it to the owners was without thievish mind and guiltless, but one who seized a blown turban before the owners could see it incurred defeat. A bhikkhu who took a rag from an undecayed corpse was pursued by the peta dwelling in it; he was not guilty of defeat, but henceforth taking rag-cloth from an undecayed body is wrong-doing. Shifting the grass-marker to get a better robe at a distribution was defeat. Ānanda, putting on another bhikkhu's inner robe in the bath-house thinking it his own, was excused as perceiving it as his own. Bhikkhus eating the leavings of lions, tigers, leopards, hyenas and wolves were excused, since these are owned by an animal. Claiming a second share of rice, food, cakes, sugarcane or timbarūsaka fruit by a groundless claim was not defeat but expiation for deliberate lying. Stealing a bowlful of rice, meat or cakes in a famine was defeat, as was stealing a bag together with the stool it lay on, a mattress of the Saṅgha, a robe on the robe-line, or a robe taken inside a dwelling—whether or not the thief left the dwelling.

Taking a friend's share on trust, or eating another's portion perceiving it as one's own, or eating from one's own bowl a portion placed there, were all guiltless. Fruit dropped by fleeing mango-, rose-apple-, breadfruit-, jackfruit-, palm-, sugarcane- and timbarūsaka-thieves could be eaten if perceived as rags from a dust-heap, but eating it with thievish mind before the owners arrived was defeat; so too stealing the Saṅgha's fruit, or flowers worth five māsakas, whether already picked or picked by oneself. Saying "I will speak in your name" or "speak in mine" to obtain goods from supporters was wrong-doing, not defeat. A bhikkhu unknowingly carrying a jewel past a toll-station was guiltless, but one bribed to smuggle a jewel through incurred defeat. Releasing a trapped pig, deer or fish out of compassion was guiltless; releasing it with thievish mind was defeat. Picking up meat dropped by a hawk to return it was guiltless, taking it thievishly was defeat; likewise scattered raft-timbers on the Aciravatī, a cowherd's cloth on a tree, and a washerman's cloth caught on one's foot in the river. Eating ghee little by little from a jar was wrong-doing. Several bhikkhus who agreed to steal together were all guilty of defeat, even though only one took the goods and even though each share was less than five māsakas. Stealing a handful of rice, beans, or sesame in the Sāvatthī famine was defeat; eating meat left by cattle- and pig-thieves in the Blind Men's Grove as rag-refuse was guiltless. Stealing grass worth five māsakas, cut or self-cut, was defeat, but incoming bhikkhus who had the Saṅgha's fruit distributed for consumption were guiltless, as were those accepting fruit given by hired watchmen. Borrowing the Saṅgha's timber temporarily was allowed, and temporary removal of furniture to the Uposatha hall was permitted, though furniture belonging to one dwelling should not be used elsewhere. Stealing the Saṅgha's water, clay, heaped grass, bed, stool, mattress, pillow, door, window or rafter was defeat, though burning its heaped grass was only wrong-doing. Thullanandā's pupils, who obtained gruel and honey-balls in their teacher's name and ate them, were guilty only of expiation for deliberate lying.

In Vesālī the venerable Ajjuka, asked by a householder to point out a hidden treasure to whichever of two boys—son or nephew—was faithful, pointed it out to the nephew. The son complained to Ānanda, who declared Ajjuka no true recluse; but Upāli, questioning Ānanda, established that one who points out a place as instructed by the owner commits no offence, not even wrong-doing, and Ajjuka was acquitted. Pilindavaccha, who brought back by psychic power two children carried off by thieves, was declared guiltless, since it was an exercise of psychic power by one possessing it. Paṇḍuka's companion, wrongly reproached as no recluse for picking up a fallen strip of fat, thought himself already fallen and consented to intercourse with a cowherd woman: he was guiltless of theft but guilty of defeat through sexual intercourse. In Sāgalā, Daḷhika's pupil who stole a shopkeeper's turban was found, on appraisal, to have taken less than five māsakas, and was acquitted and encouraged by a talk on Dhamma.

While dwelling at Vesālī in the Great Wood, the Buddha praised in many ways the development of the perception of the unattractive and the attainment based on it, then went into seclusion for a fortnight, seeing no one but the almsfood-bringer. The bhikkhus, devoting themselves to the contemplation of foulness, became ashamed and disgusted with their own bodies as a young person would be disgusted by a corpse hung round the neck; they took their own lives, killed one another, and approached Migalaṇḍika the sham recluse saying, "Deprive us of life; the bowl and robe shall be yours." Having killed many, he was seized with remorse while washing his bloody sword, until a deity of Māra's company, walking on the water, praised him as ferrying across those not yet crossed; thereafter he went from dwelling to dwelling asking, "Who is not crossed over? Whom shall I ferry across?" Those still with passion felt fear and terror, the passionless did not. He killed one, two, three, up to sixty bhikkhus in a day. Emerging from seclusion, the Buddha asked Ānanda why the Saṅgha seemed so thinned; Ānanda reported what had happened and asked for another method by which the bhikkhus might be established in knowledge. The Buddha had the bhikkhus assembled and taught concentration through mindfulness of breathing as peaceful, sublime, unadulterated, a pleasant abiding which at once banishes evil unwholesome states, as a great unseasonable rain-cloud disperses the dust of the last month of summer: a bhikkhu goes to the forest, the root of a tree or an empty hut, sits cross-legged with body erect and mindfulness established before him, breathes in and out mindfully, knows long and short breaths, and trains in experiencing the whole body, calming the bodily formation, experiencing rapture, pleasure, the mental formation and its calming, experiencing the mind, gladdening, concentrating and liberating it, and contemplating impermanence, fading away, cessation and relinquishment.

Then, rebuking the bhikkhus for taking their own and each other's lives, the Buddha laid down: whatever bhikkhu intentionally deprives a human being of life or seeks out a knife-bringer for him is one defeated, not in communion. Later the group of six, lusting after the beautiful wife of a sick lay-follower, praised the beauty of death to him, telling him that having done good and no evil, death was better than this wretched life and would bring him to heaven and celestial pleasures; persuaded, he ate unsuitable food, fell gravely ill and died, and his widow and other people complained that the Sakyan recluses were shameless liars who had killed her husband. The rule was accordingly expanded to include praising the beauty of death or inciting to death—"Good man, what use is this wretched evil life to you? Death is better for you"—with such intention and thought, in various ways.

The word-analysis explains: "intentionally" means knowingly, deliberately, with transgression; "human being" means what arises in the mother's womb from the first consciousness until the time of death; "deprives of life" means cutting off the life-faculty, breaking the continuity; "knife-bringer" covers sword, spear, club, stone, poison or rope; "praises the beauty of death" means showing the danger in life and praising death; "incites to death" means saying "bring a knife, eat poison, hang yourself." One so defeated is like a great stone broken in two, irreparable, no recluse, no son of the Sakyans; "not in communion" means excluded from a single formal act, a single recitation, an equal training.

There follows the casuistry: killing oneself, by command, by messenger, by relayed messengers, by a messenger who goes astray or returns, speaking openly or in private with correct or mistaken perception, praising death by body, by speech, by both, by messenger, or by writing; and by pit, prop, laying down a weapon, medicine, or the presentation of sights, sounds, smells, tastes, touches and teachings, by telling, by instructing, by making an appointed sign and by making a signal. Commanding another to kill is wrong-doing; if the killer kills the intended person, both are defeated; if, thinking of him, he kills another, the original instigator is guiltless and the killer defeated; and so on through the permutations. In a chain of messengers each transmission is wrong-doing, the instigator incurs a grave offence when the assassin accepts, and all are defeated when the deed is done; but if the message is passed to someone other than the one named, the original instigator is guiltless. If the instigator repents but does not recall the order, both are defeated; if he recalls it and the other kills anyway, only the killer is defeated; if the other desists, neither is guilty. Idle exclamations that so-and-so should be killed are wrong-doing. In each method—praise of death by gesture or word, digging a pit for a designated or undesignated victim, smearing a support with poison, laying down a weapon, giving ghee or honey, presenting frightful or delightful objects of the senses, telling a hell-story to the evil-doer or a heaven-story to the good-doer, answering when asked or instructing unasked, fixing a time or a bodily sign—the preliminary act is wrong-doing, the arising of painful feeling a grave offence, and death an offence entailing defeat. If a yakkha, peta or non-human falls into an undesignated pit and dies, the offence is grave; if an animal, expiation. There is no offence for one who does not act intentionally, who does not know, who does not intend death, for one who is mad, or for the first offender.
